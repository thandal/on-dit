# How often ne is dropped

**Question.** The "More: ne" box in 7.1 says that the fuller negative with *ne* (*je ne sais pas*, "I don't know") is "almost always left out" in everyday speech, and that you'll hear it "in formal speech, on the news, and when someone is being emphatic or careful". How often do people in France actually say *ne*, and what makes them say it?

**Answer.** The box is right, and the published research agrees on it unusually well. In informal speech recorded in France over the last few decades, *ne* turns up in roughly 2–10% of negations: 7% in Roubaix, under 2% among Lorraine teenagers, about 8% in the Paris region, 8.5% in parents' speech to small children, 5.4% among young adults in Tours in 2024. A generation or two earlier it was much commoner (37% in Tours in 1976, over 50% among educated Parisians in the 1960s), and it is still common in public speech: 72% in a serious radio discussion programme in 1997, about two-thirds for politicians interviewed on TV. Within any one setting, the subject matters most: after a full noun (*mon frère ne…*, "my brother doesn't…") *ne* is kept far more often than after a pronoun, and after *c'est* ("it's") almost never. The commonest phrases (*c'est pas*, *je sais pas*, *il y a pas*, *il faut pas*) hardly ever have it. Younger speakers use it less. Class, education and sex mattered in 1970s data but make little consistent difference in recent studies. A count in the CEFC-ORFEO transcripts agrees: *ne* is written in 5% of negations in everyday conversation, 15% in interviews and 50–65% on radio and TV, in lectures and in storytelling performances.

**For the grammar.** 7.1 stands as written. One sentence could be added to the box, since it's the pattern a learner will notice: *ne* turns up most after a noun subject (*mon frère ne sait pas*, "my brother doesn't know") and almost never after *je* or *c'est*. The note in 7.2 that careful speakers often keep *ne* when *personne* or *rien* is the subject (*personne n'est venu*, "nobody came") also holds: about half the time in the transcript count. *de pas venir* ("not to come") in 7.1 needs no change, though *ne* is somewhat more frequent before an infinitive than with a finite verb.

## What the research says

Studies of *ne* count negations that could have it (*je (ne) sais pas*, *il (n')y a rien*) and report the share where it is said. Most authors recorded and transcribed their own speakers. Most leave out *on* + vowel, where *on n'a pas* and *on‿a pas* ("we don't have") sound the same.

Outside France, for comparison: Montreal 0.5% (Sankoff & Vincent 1980), Swiss conversation 2.5% ([Fonseca-Greber 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269507002992)), French celebrities on Belgian radio 50% (Moreau 1986). Blanche-Benveniste (1997) sums up everyday conversation in France as about 95% without *ne*, at all ages and in all social groups.

**Over time.** Every real-time comparison shows a fall. Tours went from 36.6% (1976) to 15.7% (1995) with the same interview design, and to 5.4% among young adults in 2024. The Paris region went from 15.8% (1972–74) to 8.2% (1989–93). The radio went from 92.6% (1960–61) to 72.5% (1997).

**The numbers aren't directly comparable.** They range from 1% to 93% because the studies sampled different people in different situations: teenagers at school, adults talking to a foreign researcher, experts on national radio ([Meisner, Robert-Tissot & Stark 2015](https://doi.org/10.34847/nkl.dc54rol2), §3.2.2). The figures that matter for a learner are the ones for informal conversation among adults: 5–10%.

### What makes ne more likely

**The situation.** The biggest single effect. The same speakers who drop *ne* with friends use it in formal settings.

- Coveney recorded one camp director in two sittings. Explaining the camp's philosophy in the evening, with a colleague present, he used *ne* 50% of the time; chatting the next morning while walking round the grounds, 11%.
- Ashby (1981) recorded three speakers at work and at home with their families after a meal: 35% at work, 16% at home.
- Some pupils from the Paris suburbs recorded by Meisner used almost no *ne* with friends and up to 50% in an oral exam. Swiss students didn't shift (EGF notice, §3.2.3).
- Barcat compared three settings: friends and family, almost no *ne*; politicians interviewed on TV, *ne* in roughly 60–70%; the same politicians reading a speech aloud, nearly always.
- In the CRFP, a reference corpus of spoken French recorded around 2000, *ne* is in 14% of negations in informal recordings and 33% in formal ones (*Grande Grammaire du français*, as quoted by [Nadeau 2023](https://lactualite.com/societe/notre-pauvre-ne/)).

**The subject.** Every study finds the same ranking, and [Meisner (2016)](https://directory.doabooks.org/handle/20.500.12854/34516) calls it the most important linguistic factor: nouns and names keep *ne* most, then *qui* ("who") and *ça* ("that"), then subject pronouns, and *ce* (as in *c'est*) least. Coveney's figures:

| subject | *ne* kept |
| --- | ---: |
| a noun | 67.2% |
| none, infinitive (*de ne pas faire*) | 62.5% |
| *qui* | 44.3% |
| *ça* | 29.7% |
| *vous* | 27.9% |
| *on* | 15.9% |
| *il* | 12.2% |
| *je* | 11.5% |
| *tu* | 10.4% |
| *ce* | 2.3% |

On the radio, *ne* after nouns barely fell between 1960 and 1997 (98.5% to 92.4%) while *ne* after pronouns fell steeply, so the gap widened (Armstrong & Smith). In Meisner's data *ne* is almost always kept after a noun subject and seems to be missing altogether after a doubled subject like *moi je* (EGF notice, §3.1). Meisner & Pomino find *ne* never occurs with the reduced forms of *je* ([ʃ], [ʒ], as in *chais pas*) or *il* ([i]) ([abstract](https://www.uzh.ch/cmsssl/dam/jcr:ffffffff-942f-8416-ffff-ffffc7cde767/Meisner_Pomino_Abstract_NegClit2012.pdf)). The usual explanations: pronoun + verb is so frequent that the whole group wears down, and a heavy subject slows the speaker and makes them more careful (Coveney) or protects *ne* rhythmically (Meisner). Text messages and online chat show the same subject effect ([van Compernolle 2008](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954394508000112); Stark 2012, cited in the EGF notice).

**Fixed phrases and frequent verbs.** The commonest subject + verb combinations lose *ne* most. Ashby (1981) found *ne* dropped more often in *il (n')y a pas*, *je (ne) sais pas*, *il (ne) faut pas* and *ce (n')est pas* than in all other negations together. In Coveney's data *c'est* had *ne* 3.6% of the time, *je sais* 8.6%, *j'ai* 8.7%, *il y a* 2.4%, *c'était* and *il y avait* 0%; frequent sequences as a whole 10.1%, against 18.8% overall. Barcat found the full standard pronunciation in only 0.3–1% of ten very frequent chunks (*c'est, c'était, il y a, il faut, je sais, je suis*…). Moreau (1986) found *je (ne) vais pas* the most reduced in her radio data.

**The negative word.** A smaller effect. *pas*, the commonest, keeps *ne* least, and *que* ("only") most. Coveney: *que* 34.9%, *personne* 33.3%, *jamais* 26.2%, *plus* 25.8%, *rien* 21.2%, *pas* 16.4%. Radio 1997: *que* 95%, *jamais* 88%, *rien* 81%, *plus* 78%, *pas* 71%.

**Clauses without a subject.** *ne* is kept much more before an infinitive (*pour ne pas tomber*, "so as not to fall"): 62.5% against 18.8% overall for Coveney, and 94% on the radio in 1997, almost unchanged since 1960. With *personne* or *rien* as the subject, Barcat found little deletion.

**Sounds.** Coveney noticed *ne* is kept more where dropping it would leave two vowels side by side (*qui (n')a*, *qui (n')ont*, over 50%) or call for a liaison (*ils (n')ont*, 44%). When *ne* is said, it's often just [n] with no vowel (Barcat). Bonafede also finds the sound context matters.

**Emphasis and seriousness.** [Fonseca-Greber (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269507002992) argues that in Swiss conversation, where *ne* is very rare, what's left marks emphasis. [van Compernolle (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1473-4192.2009.00213.x) describes emphatic *ne* in informal French: said slowly, every syllable stressed, with small pauses, *je - ne - veux - pas*. Bonafede finds both emphasis and a serious topic raise *ne* in Tours. In Montreal, the few *ne* left cluster in serious topics such as religion and education (Sankoff & Vincent 1980).

**Age.** Younger speakers use *ne* less in every study that compares ages (EGF notice, §3.2.2). Coveney: speakers aged 17–22 averaged 8.4%, aged 24–37 23.9%. In Ashby's 1976 Tours data, older middle- and upper-class speakers kept *ne* 60–75% of the time. Barcat finds the youngest drop it most, though the oldest don't keep it most.

**Class, education and sex.** These mattered in the older data and have faded. In Tours in 1976, social class made a difference and men kept *ne* more than women (42% against 30%); by 1995 men and women were both at 16% (Ashby 2001). Coveney found working-class speakers lower (9% against 16–19%), but they were also his youngest. Barcat finds the social differences minor and impossible to generalize. Even the direction isn't stable: working-class speakers kept *ne* most in Ashby's 1976 Paris data and least in his 1995 Tours data (EGF notice, §3.2.2).

**Region.** *ne* has been reported higher in the south (65.7% in Béarn in 1975) than in the north (Somme, Roubaix, Lorraine), but few studies cover the regions and most are old (EGF notice, §3.2.1).

### Where studies disagree

- **Change or age.** Ashby (2001) reads the falling rates as a change in progress: each generation drops *ne* more. Others think much of the age difference is age-grading: young people drop *ne* and use it more once they work in formal settings (Blanche-Benveniste & Jeanjean 1987; Posner 2007). Pooley warns that his 1983 and 1995 samples are adults and teenagers, so the fall between them may be partly age. A third view is that dropping *ne* has been common in speech since at least the 17th century and is changing only slowly (Dufter & Stark 2007, from a doctor's record of the young Louis XIII's speech; Poplack & St-Amand 2009 for Quebec).
- **One grammar or two.** Some argue that informal French has its own grammar, in which *je, tu, il* are prefixes on the verb and *ne* has no place, and that speakers switch between it and standard French (Culbertson 2010; Massot 2010; Zribi-Hertz 2011). Meisner & Pomino (2014) and the EGF notice argue against: rates form a continuum from 0% to 100%, and every situation shows some variation.
- **Emphatic *ne*.** Fonseca-Greber and van Compernolle treat it as a real function. The EGF notice holds that most remaining *ne* depends on rhythm and the subject, with only occasional clearly emphatic cases in Meisner's corpus.
- **Social factors.** Older studies find class and education effects, newer ones mostly don't (above).

## Corpus check (transcripts only)

As a supporting check, [`extract.py`](extract.py) counts every negated verb in the spoken part of [CEFC-ORFEO](https://www.ortolang.fr/market/corpora/cefc-orfeo) recorded in France (about 1,600 speakers plus 2,000 calls to a transport help line; see [`../README.md`](../README.md)) and whether the transcript writes *ne*. These are transcribers' spellings, not checked against what was said. An audio check of a sample was started and dropped; no results from it are reported.

Two cautions:

- **Transcribers fill in an *n'* they can't hear.** After *on* + vowel they write *on n'a pas* 66% of the time, against *ne* in 14% of *on* + consonant (*on (ne) sait pas*). One corpus, CRFP, writes *on n'* before 98% of vowels. These cases are left out of everything below ([`results/on_vowel.csv`](results/on_vowel.csv)).
- **They may normalize elsewhere too.** CRFP also has the highest *ne* rates of the interview corpora, so its figures may be inflated.

Also left out: *pas mal* ("quite a lot"), *plus* where it could mean "more", and inverted questions. Of 70 hits checked by hand, 69 are clear negations. That leaves 43,046 negations.
