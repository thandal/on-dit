# Literature survey: where speech differs from the grammar

**Question.** What does published research on everyday spoken French in France already know about where speech differs from the written standard, especially where it is simpler than learners are taught, and which of those findings should the grammar act on?

**Answer.** Most of what the grammar teaches holds up: questions by intonation, *on* for "we", no inversion, the verb forms built from stems. The literature points to three changes (*c'est quoi ?* as the default "what is it?", *faut* without *il* as a relaxed form, no tie after *est*), two claims to soften (topic first as "the normal way", *que* "never" left out), and a few "you'll hear" notes (*j'ai été* for "I went", *je crois* without *que*, *tu* for "people in general"). Several topics have no usable published numbers and need a corpus count.

This survey leaves out the questions already studied in this folder: [`ils-stems/`](../ils-stems/), [`ne/`](../ne/), [`participles/`](../participles/), [`dont/`](../dont/), [`subjunctive/`](../subjunctive/) and [`futur/`](../futur/). Numbers are as the studies report them. Where I could read only an abstract or another author's summary, the entry says so.

## 1. Question words and *c'est quoi*

**Findings.** The largest count from conversation in France is Lefeuvre's: 699 question-word questions from family meals in the Orléans corpus (ESLO2, recorded around 2012).

| type | example | share |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| question word in place | *et Jacky il a pris quoi ?* | 44% |
| no verb | *quand ?*, *comment ça ?* | 30% |
| question word first, normal order | *pourquoi tu l'écoutes ?* | 11% |
| *est-ce que* | *où est-ce que…* | 8% |
| inversion, or the question word as subject | *comment fais-tu ?*, *qui veut du pain ?* | 4% |
| cleft | *où c'est qu'on éteint ?* | 3% |

Leaving out the questions with no verb, 63% have the question word in place. Three details matter for the grammar:

- **"What is it?"** *c'est quoi* is "by far" the commonest in-place question. It turns up 68 times against 5 for *qu'est-ce que c'est*. The grammar doesn't teach *c'est quoi* anywhere. *c'est quoi, ça ?* ("what's that?") and *c'est quoi ton numéro ?* ("what's your number?") should be the default.
- **"What" with another verb** is split evenly: 67 *qu'est-ce que tu fais ?* against 62 *tu fais quoi ?*. 8.3 already presents them as equals.
- **comment and pourquoi go first.** *pourquoi* never appears in place; *comment* goes first 57 times and stays in place 5 times. 8.2 says only *pourquoi* "usually goes first", and gives *ça s'écrit comment ?* as a model. Both should be named.

For yes/no questions, a review of the studies of France finds intonation alone "largely" the commonest type, *est-ce que* "around 10%", and complex inversion (*Paul vient-il ?*) "almost nonexistent" (Dagnac 2013). Simple inversion survives in routines (*quelle heure est-il ?*), and even there *il est quelle heure ?* competes. In child-directed speech recorded in Lyon, inversion is 0.1% of yes/no questions and 1.4% of question-word questions (Culbertson 2010, as cited by Liang et al. 2024). This matches [8.1](../../grammaire/08-questions.html#s8-1), [8.5](../../grammaire/08-questions.html#s8-5) and [A0](../../grammaire/annexe.html#a0). Dagnac adds one point the grammar could use: *est-ce que* doesn't work for a request with a plain verb (*tu me passes le livre ?* is fine, *est-ce que tu me passes le livre ?* isn't), but works with *pouvoir* (*est-ce que tu peux me passer le livre ?*).

**Where studies disagree.** Older counts find far fewer in-place questions: about 13% in Coveney (1995), as quoted by Ledegen (2024). Summaries of Quillard (2000) give 15.6% (Ledegen 2024) or 41.6% (Lefeuvre 2020), probably with different denominators. Adli (2015) has 57.6% (as cited by Lefeuvre 2020), and an editorial overview of the field says in-place questions can reach "nearly 60%" in informal European corpora (Guryev, Dekhissi & Bonan 2024). The differences come from the kind of recording (interviews have few questions from the interviewee; meals and phone calls have many), from what counts as a question, and from change over time: in-place questions are rare before the 19th century and spread through the 20th, and young speakers use them most. Lefeuvre's 63% includes *c'est quoi*, so for *où, quand, combien* with an ordinary verb the share is not known. A [count per question word](#where-a-corpus-count-would-help) would settle it.

**Where from.** Orléans and Lyon (France) for the main figures; the older counts and the editorial also draw on Switzerland and Quebec.

## 2. *faut* with no *il*

**Findings.** Widera (2022) compares the two Orléans surveys, ESLO1 (1968–71) and ESLO2 (2008), for *il* before *falloir*. I could read only the abstract; the figures are from Leis's (2024) summary of it.

- *il* is left out in 57% of main clauses, 21% of other subordinate clauses and 8% of relative clauses. Leis doesn't say whether this is both surveys together or 2008 alone.
- It's a change in progress. Young speakers left it out 16% of the time in 1968–71 and 66% in 2008. In 2008 every social group except the highest leaves it out more than half the time.
- The same omission is reported with *paraît (que)*, *vaut mieux*, *suffit*, *s'agit*, *(ça) fait* + adjective and *y a* (Zimmermann & Kaiser 2014, as listed by Leis). Weather verbs keep *il*: *il pleut*.
- In Swiss conversation, *il* is never said with *y avoir* (0 of 510) and is left out with *falloir* 44% of the time (Fonseca-Greber 2004, as cited by Leis). Swiss, so only supporting evidence.

**For the grammar.** [1.7](../../grammaire/01-sons.html#s1-7) treats *il faut* → "i faut" as a regular reduction. The literature says the *il* is more often not there at all in a main clause: *faut y aller* ("we've got to go"), *faut que je parte* ("I have to go"), *paraît qu'il pleut* ("apparently it's raining"), *vaut mieux pas* ("better not"). That is the same move the grammar already makes for *y a*. Suggest adding *faut* (and *paraît*, *vaut mieux*) to the relaxed forms in 1.7 and using *faut* in casual examples, while keeping *il faut* after *que*, *qui* and in careful speech.

**Caveat.** This is one city's transcripts. Widera notes that for *y a* a reduced "i" can't be told from no *il* at all, and the same risk applies, less strongly, to *faut*: a transcriber may write *faut* for a very short "i faut", or *il faut* for *faut*. A [count with an audio check](#where-a-corpus-count-would-help) would confirm the rate for France as a whole.

## 3. Linking after *est*, and other optional ties

**Findings.** The PFC project codes every possible liaison in its recordings. Mallet's thesis gives rates per word from the PFC conversations (24 surveys, 259 speakers; mostly France, with some from Belgium, Switzerland, Réunion and Abidjan):

| first word | made | of | rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| *très* | 140 | 145 | 97% |
| *dans* | 378 | 398 | 95% |
| *tout* | 238 | 287 | 83% |
| *chez* | 53 | 64 | 83% |
| *quand* | 498 | 639 | 78% |
| *petit* | 36 | 48 | 75% |
| *plus* | 159 | 248 | 64% |
| *grand* | 5 | 9 | (56%) |
| *est* | 279 | 636 | 44% |
| *c'est* | 413 | 1,470 | 28% |
| *sont* | 40 | 208 | 19% |
| *suis* | 58 | 430 | 13% |
| *ont* | 17 | 178 | 10% |
| *était* | 29 | 343 | 9% |
| *pas* | 12 | 880 | 1% |
| *mais* | 4 | 791 | 1% |

The obligatory ones (*les*, *des*, *ils*, *vous*, *on*, *deux*) are made 97–100% of the time. For *est allé(e)* specifically, Durand et al. (2011) report 15 of 15 with liaison in the south and 11 of 18 in the north. Across all sites, conversation makes 43% of possible liaisons against 59% when reading aloud, and the youngest speakers make the fewest. In the casual talk of young Parisian students (an automatic study of 46 speakers), only about 1 optional liaison in 9 is made, though that study counts many sites the PFC wouldn't (Adda-Decker et al. 2012).

**For the grammar.** Most ties in the grammar are on obligatory liaisons, or on *très*, *dans*, *quand*, *chez*, *tout* and *plus*, which are made most of the time. The exception is *est*: the grammar ties *il est‿allé*, *il est‿arrivé*, *il est‿entré*, *elle est‿arrivée* (twice) and *la porte est‿ouverte*. After *est*, conversation makes the liaison less than half the time, which is not "very common" by the README's own rule. Suggest writing these without a tie, and adding one sentence to [1.6](../../grammaire/01-sons.html#s1-6): after verbs (*est*, *c'est*, *sont*, *ont*, *suis*), linking is optional and usually skipped in conversation. 1.6's line "the tie is always shown, so you never have to guess" can stay if the ties left are only the near-certain ones. *grand‿appartement* rests on 9 tokens and can stay as written.

**Where from.** Mostly France; Durand et al. find north and south alike except on a few sequences.

## 4. *j'ai été* for "I went"

**Findings.** In compound tenses, *aller* has two forms: *je suis allé à Lyon* and *j'ai été à Lyon*, both "I went to Lyon", also before an infinitive (*j'ai été le voir*, "I went to see him"). Prescriptive sources call *avoir été* familiar, and Word's spellchecker flags it, but speakers in France use it a lot.

- In the spoken part of CEFC-ORFEO (2,099 cases, all countries), Thuilier et al. (2025, abstract) find *aller* 61.5% and *être* 38.5%. By country: France 69.7% *aller*, so about 30% *avoir été*; Switzerland 50%; Belgium 72% *avoir été*. With *y* (*j'y suis allé*), *aller* is 87%.
- An anonymised manuscript from what seems to be the same team, on the 1,157 cases with full speaker data, splits France by region: *avoir été* 41% in Île-de-France, 54% in the north and east, 19% in the south and west. Speakers over 60 use it more (55%) than speakers aged 16–20 (37%); men more than women (49% against 38%).
- Glikman & Patard (2022) report 53% *être* in their spoken European data (507 cases); an Ontario study has 63% (Sammons, Nadasdi & Mougeon 2015, Canada).

No linguistic factor separates the two cleanly. The choice is mostly social and regional.

**For the grammar.** A "you'll hear" note in [5.4](../../grammaire/05-passe-futur.html#s5-4) and in the [être list](../../grammaire/listes.html#etre): *j'ai été à Paris la semaine dernière* = *je suis allé à Paris*, "I went to Paris last week". The form to produce stays *je suis allé*, since many people notice and dislike *j'ai été* and the south uses it little.

## 5. *que* after *je crois*, *je pense*

**Findings.** Ferreira & da Cunha (2024) is the first quantitative study of this in France. They hand-coded every *croire* and *penser* followed by a clause, with or without *que* (*je pense qu'il vient* / *je pense il vient*, "I think he's coming"), and in MPF checked the audio for a swallowed *que*.

| corpus | *croire* without *que* | *penser* without *que* | all |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Multicultural Paris French (young people, Paris suburbs) | 134 of 373 (36%) | 57 of 866 (7%) | 15% |
| CEFC-ORFEO (adults; France, Switzerland, Belgium) | 70 of 2,625 (3%) | 156 of 2,408 (6%) | 4% |

Within CEFC the Swiss subcorpus is at 14%; the others (France and Belgium) range from 1% to 7%. Leaving out *que* is commoner after *je* than other subjects, almost absent when the first verb is negative (3%), commoner after other hedges (*j'en sais rien, je crois c'est pour se reposer*), and commoner in close, relaxed talk (8% in the most distant MPF recordings, about 20% in the others). Blanche-Benveniste (1989) treats these verbs as "weak" heads that behave like asides; the same alternation is well studied in English (*I think it's closed*) and Quebec.

**For the grammar.** [10.4](../../grammaire/10-relier.html#s10-4) says "French never leaves out *que*" and [10.6](../../grammaire/10-relier.html#s10-6) says "*que* is never left out". For relative clauses that holds. For *je crois*, *je pense* it is too strong. Suggest "almost never", plus a "you'll hear" note: *je crois c'est fermé* ("I think it's closed"), *je pense on va être en retard* ("I think we're going to be late"). Production default unchanged.

**Sources.**
- Ferreira, Auphélie & Yanis da Cunha (2024). Étude statistique sur corpus de l'alternance *que* / Ø en français parlé : quel est l'effet de la proximité communicationnelle ? *Discours* 34. [Open access](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/13127), [doi:10.4000/12jg6](https://doi.org/10.4000/12jg6). Tables 3 and 4, figures 3 and 6.
- Blanche-Benveniste, Claire (1989). Constructions verbales « en incise » et rection faible des verbes. *Recherches sur le français parlé* 9. Not seen; cited by Ferreira & da Cunha.

## 6. Topic first with a noun subject

**Findings.** The best numbers for France come from two Paris corpora:

- **CFPP2000** (interviews, adults of all ages): a noun subject is doubled by a pronoun (*mon frère, il habite à Lyon*) 22% of the time. Common nouns 20%, proper names 32%, *lui*, *eux* 70% (Zahler 2014).
- **MPF** (young people, mostly under 30, Paris suburbs): 74% of 3,543 noun subjects. Definite subjects 81%, indefinite 43%, quantified subjects like *tout le monde* ("everyone") 7%. Main clauses 78%, other subordinate clauses 62%, relative clauses 30%. Almost never when the speaker keeps *ne* (Liang, Donati & Burnett 2024).

Older studies quoted by Liang et al. give over 80% in Marseille and 96% among teenagers in Villejuif (both 1980s); I haven't checked those originals, and I found no verified figure for adults elsewhere in France. Liang et al. can't tell whether the CFPP–MPF gap is age, setting (interview against peer talk) or bilingualism.

**For the grammar.** [9.1](../../grammaire/09-forme.html#s9-1) calls topic first "the normal way to bring up a topic, not an emphatic one", and says plain order "sounds flatter". That fits young speakers and stressed pronouns (*moi, je…* is near-automatic). For adults in interviews, plain order is still about four times in five. Suggest: keep the section, but say both orders are normal with a noun subject, that doubling is almost automatic with *moi, toi, lui*, and that it's rare with *tout le monde* ("everyone"), *personne* ("nobody") and *quelqu'un* ("someone"): *tout le monde est là* ("everyone's here"), rarely *tout le monde il est là*. A [count by age in ORFEO](#where-a-corpus-count-would-help) would separate age from setting.

**Sources.**
- Zahler, Sara (2014). Variable subject doubling in spoken Parisian French. *University of Pennsylvania Working Papers in Linguistics* 20(1): 360–371. Figures as cited by Liang et al.
- Liang, Yiming, Caterina Donati & Heather Burnett (2024). French subject doubling: a third path. *Isogloss* 10(7): 1–28. [hal-04841794](https://hal.science/hal-04841794).
- Coveney, Aidan (2005). Subject doubling in spoken French: a sociolinguistic approach. *The French Review* 79(1): 96–111. Not seen.

## 7. Questions inside a sentence

**Findings.**

- In CFPP2000 (602,000 words of Paris interviews), after *je (ne) sais pas* the normal order (*je sais pas où il est*) beats the *est-ce que* type (*je sais pas où est-ce que je vais*) 66 to 6. The corpus has 27 indirect questions with *est-ce que* in all, from speakers of every background, including a medical student from the 7th arrondissement. *où* ("where") favours *est-ce que*; after *je sais pas*, *comment* and *pourquoi* never take it in this corpus (Branca-Rosoff & Lefeuvre 2016).
- The question word left in place (*je sais pas c'est quoi*, "I don't know what it is") turns up once in 144 indirect questions in CFPP2000 and 5 times in 98 in the ESLO meals (Lefeuvre & Rossi-Gensane 2017, via Ledegen 2024). Among young people in multiethnic Paris suburbs it is 38% (61 of 159), 57% for active bilinguals and 8% for monolinguals (Gardner-Chloros & Secova 2018). Ledegen (2024) argues it is spreading into ordinary French, with examples from radio and students.

**For the grammar.** [8.4](../../grammaire/08-questions.html#s8-4) stands as the default. A recognition note could show *je sais pas où est-ce qu'il habite* (common, unremarkable) and *je sais pas c'est quoi* (young, informal, still frowned on).

**Sources.**
- Branca-Rosoff, Sonia & Florence Lefeuvre (2016). Le CFPP2000 : constitution, outils et analyses. Le cas des interrogatives indirectes. *Corpus* 15. [doi:10.4000/corpus.3043](https://doi.org/10.4000/corpus.3043). Table 1.
- Gardner-Chloros, Penelope & Maria Secova (2018). Grammatical change in Paris French: in situ question words in embedded contexts. *Journal of French Language Studies* 28(2): 181–207. [doi:10.1017/S0959269518000091](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269518000091), [open copy](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/22079/).
- Ledegen, Gudrun (2024). The embedded Wh- in situ clause: French tout court? *Journal of French Language Studies* 34: 39–71. [doi:10.1017/S095926952400005X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095926952400005X).
- Lefeuvre, Florence & Nathalie Rossi-Gensane (2017). Not seen; figures via Ledegen.

## 8. *tu* for "people in general"

**Findings.** Ashby (1992) shows, from Tours, that *tu* and *vous* compete with *on* for "people in general" in France, and that a key factor is how salient the speaker wants to make the person referred to. Coveney (2003) studies the same thing in Picardy, where most speakers said *tu* to the interviewer, and asks whether that affects the choice; his abstract notes a "spectacular rise" of generic *tu* in Montreal. I could read only the abstracts, so I can't give the rates.

**For the grammar.** [3.3](../../grammaire/03-pronoms.html#s3-3) gives *on* as the word for "people in general", which is right as a default. A "you'll hear" note would help learners who otherwise take every *tu* as addressed to them: *quand t'es jeune, tu fais des bêtises* ("when you're young, you do silly things"). Medium confidence until the rates are checked; a [hand-coded sample](#where-a-corpus-count-would-help) would give them.

**Sources.**
- Ashby, William J. (1992). The variable use of *on* versus *tu/vous* for indefinite reference in spoken French. *Journal of French Language Studies* 2(2): 135–157. [doi:10.1017/S0959269500001277](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269500001277). Abstract only.
- Coveney, Aidan (2003). 'Anything you can do, *tu* can do better': *tu* and *vous* as substitutes for indefinite *on* in French. *Journal of Sociolinguistics* 7(2): 164–191. [doi:10.1111/1467-9481.00218](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9481.00218). Not seen.

## 9. *il*, *ils*, *elle* losing their l

**Findings.** Howard, Lemée & Regan (2006) summarise the studies of France: impersonal *il* loses its l 88% of the time in Tours (Ashby 1984) and 94% in Paris (Laks 1980); among teenagers in Lorraine it's categorical for impersonal *il* and *ils*, and *elle* loses it only 15% of the time (Armstrong 1996). In Paris *elle* loses it 33% of the time. A study reported second-hand gives the l of *il* as said in 6% of conversational cases against 96% in reading (Brognaux & Avanzi 2015, cited in a slide deck by N. Kondo; not checked).

**For the grammar.** [1.7](../../grammaire/01-sons.html#s1-7) is right that *il* and *ils* before a consonant are usually "i". It could add that *elle* sometimes becomes "è" (*è dit*, "she says"), for recognition. Low priority.

**Sources.**
- Armstrong, Nigel (1996). Variable deletion of French /l/: linguistic, social and stylistic factors. *Journal of French Language Studies* 6(1): 1–21. [doi:10.1017/S0959269500004956](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269500004956).
- Howard, Martin, Isabelle Lemée & Vera Regan (2006). The L2 acquisition of a phonological variable: the case of /l/ deletion in French. *Journal of French Language Studies* 16: 1–24. [doi:10.1017/S0959269506002298](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269506002298). Its review section and Table 3.

## 10. *on* for "we"

The literature agrees that subject *nous* has almost disappeared from informal speech (Coveney 2000), and the project's own negation count found 28 subject *nous* in all the ORFEO conversations it searched ([`ne/`](../ne/)). [3.1](../../grammaire/03-pronoms.html#s3-1) and [3.3](../../grammaire/03-pronoms.html#s3-3) stand. One related detail from [section 4](#4-jai-été-for-i-went): speakers who do say *nous* also choose *je suis allé* over *j'ai été* (20 to 4 in CEFC), which fits *nous* belonging to careful speech.

- Coveney, Aidan (2000). Vestiges of *nous* and the 1st person plural verb in informal spoken French. *Language Sciences* 22(4): 447–481. Not seen.

## 11. Verb forms built from stems

The standard morphological analysis of French conjugation (Bonami & Boyé 2002, and later work on "stem spaces") links the stems in a tree. The imparfait shares a stem with the present *nous* and *vous* forms; the present *ils* form depends on that stem; the subjunctive singular and *ils* forms depend on the present *ils* form; the subjunctive *nous* and *vous* forms depend on the imparfait. That is exactly what [5.5](../../grammaire/05-passe-futur.html#s5-5) and [6.5](../../grammaire/06-autres-temps.html#s6-5) teach (imparfait from *vous*, subjunctive from *ils*, subjunctive *vous* = imparfait *vous*), so the grammar's simplification has solid backing.

The future and conditional stem is a separate slot in that analysis. [5.2](../../grammaire/05-passe-futur.html#s5-2) and [6.3](../../grammaire/06-autres-temps.html#s6-3) build it from the infinitive, which works for most verbs but not for the vowel-shift verbs of [4.1](../../grammaire/04-present.html#s4-1): *acheter* gives *j'achèterais* ("achètrè"), *appeler* gives *j'appellerais* ("appèlrè"), not "ach'trè", "app'lrè". One line in 6.3 would cover it. (This is standard French, not a finding about speech.)

- Bonami, Olivier & Gilles Boyé (2002). Suppletion and dependency in inflectional morphology. *Proceedings of the HPSG 2001 Conference*. [PDF](https://proceedings.hpsg.xyz/article/download/496/500). Figure 1.

## 12. *du coup*

Abouda (2022) searched about 1.3 million words of the Orléans corpus and found *du coup* "marginal forty years ago" and now "a new discourse marker in vogue", tracking its rise in real and apparent time. I could read only the abstract; a press summary (not checked) says about two thirds of the recent cases come from speakers aged 15–25. [10.1](../../grammaire/10-relier.html#s10-1) calls *du coup* "the everyday word" for "so"; for young and middle-aged speakers that's likely right, but *donc* and *alors* may still lead among older adults. A [count by age](#where-a-corpus-count-would-help) would say.

## Claims in the grammar the literature contradicts

- **10.4 and 10.6: "*que* is never left out."** After *je crois* and *je pense* it is, 1–7% of the time in adult corpora from France and 15% among young Parisians ([5](#5-que-after-je-crois-je-pense)).
- **1.6 and the ties on *est‿allé*, *est‿arrivée*, *est‿ouverte*.** Liaison after *est* is made 44% of the time in conversation; the README reserves ties for obligatory and very common liaisons ([3](#3-linking-after-est-and-other-optional-ties)).
- **1.7: "*il faut* is often 'i faut'."** It understates: in Orléans the *il* is absent altogether in most main clauses ([2](#2-faut-with-no-il)).
- **8.2: the question word goes "where the answer would be", and only *pourquoi* usually goes first.** *comment* goes first too, 57 times in 62 ([1](#1-question-words-and-cest-quoi)).
- **9.1: topic first is "the normal way to bring up a topic".** True for young speakers and stressed pronouns; for adults in interviews, about four noun subjects in five are not doubled ([6](#6-topic-first-with-a-noun-subject)).
- **6.3: the conditionnel is the infinitive + "rè".** Not for *acheter*, *appeler*, *jeter* ([11](#11-verb-forms-built-from-stems)).

Omissions rather than contradictions: *c'est quoi* (8.3), *j'ai été* (5.4), generic *tu* (3.3).

## Where a corpus count would help

All of these can be run on the CEFC-ORFEO transcripts recorded in France with [`orfeo.py`](../orfeo.py), labelled transcript-only as the [method](../README.md#method) asks.

1. **faut with and without il.** Every present, imperfect, conditional and future form of *falloir* (*faut, fallait, faudrait, faudra*). For each, whether *il* (or *i*) comes before it. Split by clause (main; after *que*, *qui*, *si*, *quand*, *comme*), setting and age band. Do the same for *paraît*, *vaut mieux*, *suffit*, *s'agit*. Then score a sample on the audio with the [`ils-stems/`](../ils-stems/) method, since a very short "i" may be written either way.
2. **Question words.** In conversation only, for each of *où, quand, comment, combien, pourquoi, qui* and "what": in place (*tu vas où*), first with normal order (*où tu vas*), first with *est-ce que*, inversion. Also *c'est quoi* against *qu'est-ce que c'est*. Transcripts don't reliably mark questions, so draw 50 hits per word and code them by hand.
3. **tu before a vowel.** *t'* against *tu* before vowel-initial verbs, per verb (*as, es, avais, étais* against *habites, arrives, aimes, attends, vas‿y*…), per subcorpus. Check each subcorpus's transcription rules first (some may write *tu as* for "t'a"), then sample the audio. If *t'* is the norm before any vowel, 1.7 could treat it as a regular reduction, like the light e.
4. **Subject doubling by age.** Noun subjects followed by a coreferent *il, elle, ils, elles* against plain noun subjects, by age band and setting, conversation against interviews. Separates age from setting, which Liang et al. couldn't.
5. **le, la, les before lui, leur.** Count *le lui, la lui, les lui, le leur…* For *donner, prêter, rendre, montrer, envoyer, passer, dire* with *lui* or *leur* and no object after the verb, code 200 random cases as "object understood" or "no object meant".
6. **Generic tu.** 300 random *tu* from conversation and interviews, coded as addressed to the listener or meaning people in general; with generic *on* from the same recordings for comparison.
7. **du coup, donc, alors.** Each as "so" per 10,000 words, by age band (16–20, 21–60, 61+) and setting.
8. **c'est against ce sont.** Before a plural noun phrase (*les, des, mes, ces, nos, leurs*, a number), and *c'était* against *c'étaient*, by setting. Safe in transcripts, since the forms sound different.
9. **en vélo against à vélo** (and *en moto*, *à moto*), to check 13.4's "just as often".

Not needed: *j'ai été* (already counted in CEFC by Thuilier et al.), liaison (needs audio; the PFC numbers above are enough), *que* after *croire*, *penser* (Ferreira & da Cunha).
