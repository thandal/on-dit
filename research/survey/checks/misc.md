## (a) *j'ai été* for "I went" (5.4)

**Verdict: SOLID.** The survey relied on a conference abstract and an anonymised manuscript. Glikman & Patard (2022), a peer-reviewed proceedings paper read in full, and a corpus count both point the same way. The rate depends on method (whether ambiguous "been somewhere" cases count), region, age and situation. The France-wide figures all come to roughly a third of cases, and Paris interviews reach a half.

**What the primary sources say.**

- Thuilier, Juliette, Christophe Benzitoun, Gilles Corminboeuf & Gabriel Thiberge (2025). Alternance entre *avoir été* et *être allé* en français parlé. Colloque *Nouveaux regards sur la norme*. Abstract [hal-05265414](https://univ-tlse2.hal.science/hal-05265414) (read; there is no full text).
  - **Data:** the spoken part of CEFC-ORFEO (France, Switzerland, Belgium). Every compound tense, including *y* and no complement, sorted by hand.
  - **Numbers:** of 2,099 cases, 1,291 *aller* (61.5%) and 808 *être* (38.5%). Share of *aller* by country: France 69.7%, Switzerland 50.4%, Belgium 28.1%. With *y* (*j'y suis allé*), *aller* 87.3%. Public storytelling, 76.8% *aller*. **Matches the survey.**
- [Anonymised] The conditioning of overabundance: a probabilistic grammar perspective on French ALLER in periphrastic tenses. Manuscript, [PDF on G. Thiberge's page](https://pages.llf-paris.fr/~gthiberge/00/papers/00_overabundance.pdf). Read in full. It is the same team's fuller study, not peer-reviewed as far as I can tell.
  - **Data:** CEFC spoken, 2,830 automatic hits. Two annotators kept the 2,058 where *aller* could replace *avoir été* (61.9% *être allé*). Of these, 1,157 come from files with full speaker data (Table 3). Region means where the speaker grew up.
  - **Table 6:** *avoir été* in Île-de-France 99 of 242 (40.9%), north and east 76 of 142 (53.5%), south and west 65 of 341 (19.1%). Belgium 66.8%, Switzerland 48.5%. France as a whole (my sum): 240 of 725, 33%. By age: 16–20 37.0% (254), 21–60 41.7% (733), 61+ 54.7% (170). By sex: men 48.8%, women 38.3%. The age and sex figures include the Belgian and Swiss speakers.
  - **Tables 5 and 7:** inanimate subjects (*ça a été mieux*, "it got better") take *avoir été* 72% of the time. *nous* takes it 3 times in 19. More speakers present means more *avoir été*: 31% with one speaker, 40% with two, 50% with three or more.
  - **Matches the survey.** One inconsistency: this manuscript gives the Ontario study as 50.1% *être allé*, while the abstract above gives 63.2% *être*.
- Glikman, Julie & Adeline Patard (2022). Être pour aller en français d'Europe. *CMLF 2022*, SHS Web of Conferences 138: 02001. [doi:10.1051/shsconf/202213802001](https://doi.org/10.1051/shsconf/202213802001), open copy [hal-04312654](https://hal.science/hal-04312654). Read in full. The survey had only its abstract.
  - **Table 2:** three CEFC interview corpora, with only a place phrase or an infinitive after the verb, ambiguous cases kept. CFPP2000 (Paris) has *aller* 108 and *être* 117 before a place phrase, and 24 and 32 before an infinitive. That is 149 of 281 *avoir été* (53%). With Swiss OFROM and Belgian CFPB, the total is 269 of 507 (53%), the figure the survey quotes. Only CFPP2000 is from France.
  - **Table 3:** an online survey asked over 5,000 people which they say "with friends or family": *hier j'ai été à la piscine* or *hier je suis allé à la piscine* ("yesterday I went to the pool"). Of the respondents who grew up in France, 30% chose *avoir été* only, 29% both, and 41% *être allé* only. Age made no clear difference. Most free comments call *j'ai été* relaxed or incorrect and *je suis allé* careful.

**Other studies.** Sammons, Nadasdi & Mougeon (2015) study Ontario, Canada (not read). No study I found contradicts the above.

**Corpus check** (transcript-only, 10 France subcorpora). The two forms are different words, so transcripts are fair evidence. I counted *avoir* + *été* against *être* + *allé(e)(s)* when an infinitive or *à/au/aux/chez/en* follows. Before an infinitive (*j'ai été voir*), 39 of 40 sampled hits were right: 116 *avoir été* to 222 *être allé* (34%). Before a place phrase the raw count is 232 to 402. About a third of those *avoir été* hits mean "been" (*j'ai été en DEUG*, "I was doing a degree"; *été en retard*, "been late"), which leaves about 27%. That makes about 30% overall, the same as Thuilier et al. Combined raw shares by subcorpus: Paris interviews (CFPP2000) 57%, Lorraine (TCOF) 60%, Paris transport help line 43%, CRFP 36%, Aix–Marseille area (CORAL-ROM) 16%, CLAPI 14%, students' casual conversations (TUFS) 15%.

**Change for the grammar.** Add a "you'll hear" note to [5.4](../../../grammaire/05-passe-futur.html#s5-4), and a line under the [être list](../../../grammaire/listes.html#etre). Many speakers say *j'ai été* for "I went": *j'ai été à Paris la semaine dernière* = *je suis allé à Paris la semaine dernière* ("I went to Paris last week"). It also works before an infinitive: *j'ai été le voir* ("I went to see him"). It is commoner in Paris and the north and east than in the south, and among older speakers. Many people consider it sloppy, so say *je suis allé* yourself. With *y* it is nearly always *j'y suis allé*. Evidence: in recorded conversation from France about 30% of compound-past *aller* use *avoir été* (Thuilier et al. 2025), 53% in Paris interviews (Glikman & Patard 2022), 19% among speakers from the south and west (manuscript, Table 6).

## (b) *que* after *je crois*, *je pense* (10.4, 10.6)

**Verdict: SOLID for 10.6. 10.4 can stay.** One careful primary study covers France and checked the audio where it mattered, and the smaller studies agree. All of it is about clauses after *croire* and *penser* (and similar verbs), not relative clauses.

**What the primary source says.**

- Ferreira, Auphélie & Yanis da Cunha (2024). Étude statistique sur corpus de l'alternance *que* / Ø en français parlé : quel est l'effet de la proximité communicationnelle ? *Discours* 34. [doi:10.4000/12jg6](https://doi.org/10.4000/12jg6). Read in full from an archived copy of [the OpenEdition page](https://journals.openedition.org/discours/13127); OpenEdition's bot filter blocks direct access.
  - **Data:** MPF (Multicultural Paris French, about 1.2 million words): young people in multiethnic neighbourhoods and suburbs of Paris. Recordings are rated A (distant interviews), B (interviews between acquaintances) or C (unprompted talk). They took every *croire* and *penser* followed by a clause, separated true *que*-less clauses from asides like *ça a duré je pense quatre ans*, and listened to the recording (with a spectrogram) when a *que* might have been swallowed. Doubtful cases were dropped (§3.2). For comparison they used CEFC spoken (France, Switzerland, Belgium, about 3.1 million tokens) with the same method.
  - **Table 3 (MPF):** without *que*, *croire* 134 of 373 (36%), *penser* 57 of 866 (7%), all 191 of 1,239 (15%).
  - **Table 4 (CEFC, three countries):** *croire* 70 of 2,625 (3%), *penser* 156 of 2,408 (6%), all 226 of 5,033 (4%).
  - **Figure 6 (by subcorpus, no *que*):** CLAPI 7.1% (14 cases), TUFS 6.3% (66), TCOF 6.2% (26), FLEURON 4.2% (1), CRFP 3.3% (14), work meetings 2.9% (8), CFPP2000 2.5% (18), CORAL-ROM 2.2% (5), storytelling 0% (0). Swiss OFROM 13.9% (48), Belgian CFPB 4.7% and VALIBEL 1.6%. My sum for the French subcorpora: about 150 of 3,300, 4–5%.
  - **Factors** (MPF model, §4.1): more often after *je* (16% against 13%); 3% when the first verb is negative, against 16%; 22% after another hedge (*j'en sais rien du tout je crois c'est pour se reposer*), against 14%. By recording type: A 8%, B 21%, C 19%. In CEFC (*penser* only), the most relaxed category reaches 9%.
  - **Matches the survey,** with two wording fixes. "Young Parisians" should be "young people in multiethnic Paris neighbourhoods and suburbs". "1–7%" should be 0–7% per subcorpus, about 4–5% overall.

**Other studies.** These are known only through Ferreira's thesis ([tel-04269005](https://theses.hal.science/tel-04269005), ch. 2, read in part).

- Avanzi (2012) finds 20 *que*-less clauses among 157 cases of these verbs (France and Switzerland).
- Angot (2021) finds 15 in 190 (Nantes and Coutures, France, audio checked).
- Pustka (2010) finds 47 *que*-less complement clauses, 17 of them from Parisians, with no rate.
- Blanche-Benveniste & Willems (2007) call such cases "extrêmement rares" in France and argue that *que* tends to fuse with the verb instead (*je crois-que*).
- Liang et al. (2021) study Montreal (Canada).

All agree: it occurs in France, it is rare overall, and it is commoner in relaxed talk.

**On 10.4.** No study I read counts *que* left out of a relative clause ("the film I saw"). Ferreira & da Cunha mention related sequences with no linking word (*y a des gens ils viennent…*, "there are people who come…"; *c'est lui il comprend rien*, "he's the one who doesn't get it"), with no rates. Those belong to 9.3/9.4 if anywhere. The 10.4 sentence can stay.

**Change for the grammar.** In [10.6](../../../grammaire/10-relier.html#s10-6), replace "As with relative clauses, que is never left out" with "*que* is almost always kept: *il dit qu'il vient*". Add a "you'll hear" line: in relaxed talk, people sometimes leave it out after *je crois*, *je pense* (and *je trouve*, *j'ai l'impression*): *je crois c'est fermé* ("I think it's closed"). Hardly ever after a negative (*je pense pas que…*). Keep *que* yourself. Evidence: 0–7% in each French subcorpus of CEFC, and 15% among young people in multiethnic Paris, 36% after *croire* (Ferreira & da Cunha 2024, Figure 6 and Table 3). [10.4](../../../grammaire/10-relier.html#s10-4) stays as it is.

## (c) Topic first with a noun subject (9.1)

**Verdict: SOLID that 9.1 overstates for adults.** Two primary studies and a corpus count agree. The survey's suggested framing ("much more so among young speakers") is **NOT SOLID** on its own. Zahler found the opposite age pattern in Paris interviews, and Liang et al. found no age effect within MPF. The count below shows that the situation matters at least as much as age.

**What 9.1 says, and where it conflicts.** 9.1 says: "This is the normal way to bring up a topic, not an emphatic one. Without the topic first, *j'ai vu ce film deux fois* is correct but sounds flatter."

- For noun subjects (*mon frère, il habite à Lyon*), "the normal way" plus "sounds flatter" presents topic first as the default. Adult data don't support that. In interviews about four noun subjects in five are plain. In relaxed conversation among adults it is roughly half and half.
- The studies count all noun subjects, not only topics being "brought up". In Paris interviews, already-mentioned referents are doubled more often (29%) than new ones (18%) (Zahler, Table 2). So doubling isn't specially the way to introduce a topic.
- The object rows (*ce film, je l'ai vu*), *ça*, *lui* and *y*: I found no counts, so I can't judge them. "Not emphatic" is consistent with all the studies.

**What the primary sources say.**

- Zahler, Sara (2014). Variable subject doubling in spoken Parisian French. *U. Penn Working Papers in Linguistics* 20(1): 360–371. [Repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/pwpl/vol20/iss1/38). Read in full.
  - **Data:** CFPP2000, 17 of its 30 interviews, 13 Paris neighbourhoods. Every third-person subject before the verb that could be doubled: nouns, names, *lui*/*eux*, *quelqu'un*. 1,097 tokens.
  - **Overall:** 22% (231 of 1,053).
  - **Table 2:** with *ne* 2 of 74 (3%), negative without *ne* 36%, affirmative 23%. Main clauses 27%, adverbial clauses 14%, relative, *que*, *si* and conditional clauses 10%. Singular 29%, plural 13%. Already mentioned 29%, new 18%. Long subjects or words between subject and verb 40–55%.
  - **Table 3:** *lui*, *eux* 70%, names 32%, common nouns and other pronouns 20%.
  - **Tables 4 and 6** (763 tokens with speaker data): 56 and over 29%, 31–55 14%, under 31 11% (91 tokens). Women 24%, men 13%, young men 5%.
  - Zahler notes the interviews look fairly formal (*ne* kept in 53% of negatives).
  - **Matches the survey.**
- Liang, Yiming, Caterina Donati & Heather Burnett (2024). French subject doubling: a third path. *Isogloss* 10(7)/12. [hal-04841794](https://hal.science/hal-04841794). Read in full.
  - **Data:** MPF, 66 recordings, 790,000 words. Speakers aged 12–37 from north Paris and its suburbs, all with a multicultural family background, in informal talk between friends or acquaintances. Definite, indefinite and quantified noun subjects (no *lui*/*eux*); 3,543 tokens.
  - **Rates:** 74% doubled. Definite 81%, indefinite 43%, quantified (*tout le monde*, "everyone") 7%. Main clauses 78%, other subordinate clauses 62%, relative clauses 30%. With *ne* 0% (2% counting tokens with missing data), against 76% in affirmatives.
  - **Other factors:** commoner with frequent verbs and with words between subject and verb. University-educated speakers double less (p < .05). Age has no effect within the corpus (§4.3). The authors can't tell whether the gap with CFPP2000 comes from age, register or bilingualism.
  - **Matches the survey.**

**Other studies** (not read; figures as quoted by Zahler and Liang et al.):

- Ashby (1980): middle-class Paris, 21%.
- Coveney (2003, 2005): Picardy, 24.4%.
- Sankoff (1982): Marseille, over 80% (two speakers).
- Culbertson (2010): 80.6% in adults' speech to small children in Lyon.
- Campion (1984): 96.4% among teenagers in Villejuif, according to Zahler. Liang et al. list Campion's thesis as *Left dislocation in Montréal French*, so the Villejuif attribution is uncertain.

**Corpus check** (transcript-only). CEFC-ORFEO carries a dependency parse: a doubled subject appears as a noun attached as *periph* plus *il/elle/ils/elles* attached as subject of the same verb. I counted definite nouns and names (determiner *le, mon, ce…*, or a name; no *tout le monde*, no time phrases) against the same kinds of subject without a pronoun. Impersonal *il* (*il y a*, *il faut*) was dropped. Hand checks found 43 of 50 "doubled" hits and about 34 of 40 "plain" hits right. The errors are similar on both sides, so the shares should be roughly right.

| Subcorpus (France) | Doubled / all | % |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| TUFS (students' casual conversations) | 1,046 / 2,201 | 48 |
| CLAPI (everyday interactions) | 164 / 481 | 34 |
| TCOF (Lorraine, conversations and interviews) | 544 / 1,647 | 33 |
| Work meetings | 162 / 706 | 23 |
| CFPP2000 (Paris interviews) | 348 / 2,072 | 17 |
| DECODA (transport help line) | 186 / 1,127 | 17 |
| CRFP | 254 / 2,061 | 12 |
| CORAL-ROM | 146 / 1,478 | 10 |
| Storytelling | 213 / 2,095 | 10 |
| All | 3,073 / 13,963 | 22 |

The 17% for CFPP2000 fits Zahler's 22%, since she also counted *lui*/*eux* (70%) and names (32%).

Within the same subcorpus, younger speakers double more:

- TUFS: 16–20 53% (466), 21–60 41% (819), 61+ 28% (60).
- TCOF: 58% (186), 31% (1,175), 23% (119).
- CFPP2000: 38% (72), 19% (876), 14% (950).
- CLAPI, 21–60 only: 49% (152).

So adults double about 40–50% of definite noun subjects in relaxed conversation and 10–20% in interviews, meetings and storytelling. Teenagers chatting double more than half. The 74% in MPF is young people's peer talk.

**Change for the grammar.** Keep [9.1](../../../grammaire/09-forme.html#s9-1) and its table, but replace "This is the normal way to bring up a topic… sounds flatter" with:

> It isn't emphatic. With a noun subject both orders are normal: *mon frère, il habite à Lyon* and *mon frère habite à Lyon* ("my brother lives in Lyon"). In relaxed conversation adults put the topic first about half the time, and young people more. In more careful talk the plain order is usual.

Add three limits:

- It is rare with *tout le monde* ("everyone"), *personne* ("nobody") or *quelqu'un* ("someone"): *tout le monde est là* ("everyone's here").
- It is rare in relative clauses.
- It is practically never used together with *ne* ([7.1](../../../grammaire/07-negation.html#s7-1)).

With *moi* and *toi* the pronoun is automatic (*moi, je…*); with *lui* it usually is. Evidence: 22% of subjects doubled in Paris interviews (Zahler 2014), 74% among young people in multiethnic Paris (Liang et al. 2024), and the count above. The README's "Soften" line would then read "common in relaxed talk, more so among young speakers".

## (d) *tu* for "people in general" (3.3)

**Verdict: SOLID for a recognition note.** Every source with data from France agrees that generic *tu* is common in informal speech. The rates are thin: one 1982 interview corpus from Picardy, one 1990s conversation corpus that pools France and Switzerland, and my own single-coder sample. The two classic studies (Ashby 1992; Coveney 2003) are paywalled; I read only their abstracts.

**What the primary sources say.**

- Coveney, Aidan (2016). La quête du vernaculaire dans l'étude de la variation grammaticale. *CMLF 2016*, SHS Web of Conferences 27: 01003. [doi:10.1051/shsconf/20162701003](https://doi.org/10.1051/shsconf/20162701003). Read in full from an archived copy. It reports his own Picardy data.
  - **Data:** Somme, 1982, 30 adults aged 17–60 who worked in holiday camps, interviewed by Coveney. 22 called him *tu* and 6 *vous* (Table 1).
  - **Table 3:** speakers grouped by how often they used *tu*/*vous* to address him. The mean share of *on* for generic reference (against *tu*/*vous*) is 95.7% (6 speakers), 80.8% (6), 53.7% (7) and 21.5% (8). The unweighted mean is about 60% *on* and 40% *tu*/*vous* (my calculation).
  - He concludes that generic *tu*/*vous* was "déjà, au moment de l'enquête, bien établi dans les styles informels" (already well established in informal styles at the time). Some young speakers avoided it because of the interview situation. No Picardy speaker who called him *vous* used generic *tu*; those speakers used generic *vous*.
- Waugh, Linda R. & Bonnie Fonseca-Greber (2002). Authentic materials for everyday spoken French: corpus linguistics vs. French textbooks. *Arizona Working Papers in SLAT* 9: 114–127. [PDF](https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/jslat/article/178/galley/172/download/). Read in full.
  - **Data:** 15 informal conversations among family and friends, mid-to-late 1990s, 27 educated middle-class speakers, 194,000 words. France and Switzerland are pooled.
  - **Table 4:** generic reference by *tu* 918 (68%), *on* (including cases between "we" and "people") 414 (30.7%), *vous* 18 (1.3%).
  - **Table 5:** 918 of 2,126 *tu* (43.2%) are generic; *tu sais* and *tu vois* are left out. The Swiss speakers were further along; France isn't given separately.
- Barbéris, Jeanne-Marie (2010). « Quand t'es super bobo »… La deuxième personne générique dans le français parisien des jeunes. *CMLF 2010*. [doi:10.1051/cmlf/2010258](https://doi.org/10.1051/cmlf/2010258). Read in full.
  - **Data:** CFPP2000. Qualitative, with no counts.
  - **Findings:** one 25-year-old Paris medical student uses generic *tu* constantly: *quand t'es super bobo tu fais tes courses de base chez Lidl* ("when you're really bobo you do your basic shopping at Lidl"). A first look at the corpus suggests young speakers use it much more and older ones keep *on*.
- **Abstracts only:** Ashby (1992), Tours interviews from 1976: *on* against *tu*/*vous*, with salience of the referent as a key factor. An unverified secondary summary online gives 16 speakers, 587 tokens, *vous* 240 and *tu* 10; the interviews were in *vous*. Coveney (2003): abstract only.

**Corpus check** (transcript-only; single coder; scripts and codes kept outside the repo). The corpora are CFPP2000, CLAPI, CORAL-ROM, CRFP, TCOF, TUFS and work meetings: conversation and interviews, 2.6 million words.

- ***tu*:** 160 random subject *tu*, leaving out *tu sais* and *tu vois*. Coded 59 generic, 85 addressed (including quoted speech), 13 unclear, and 3 filler *si tu veux* (dropped). So 38% of *tu* are generic, or 46% if the unclear ones are. Waugh & Fonseca-Greber found 43%.
- ***on*:** 150 random *on*. Coded 78 "we", 31 generic, 24 between "we" and "people", 10 "someone/they", 6 unclear, and 1 *on va dire*.
- **Per million words:** generic *tu* about 3,000, generic *on* about 3,100 (5,400 counting the in-between cases). So *tu* does between a third and a half of generic reference.
- **"there is":** about a fifth of the generic *tu* are *t'as* in that sense: *après t'as le quartier de Croulebarbe* ("then there's the Croulebarbe area"), *t'as quand même des métros* ("there are metros, though").
- **Limits:** samples this size carry about ±8 points. Students' conversations (TUFS) supply nearly half of all *tu*.

**Change for the grammar.** In [3.3](../../../grammaire/03-pronoms.html#s3-3), after "people in general", add a "you'll hear" note:

> In relaxed speech *tu*, like English "you", often means anyone: *quand t'es fatigué, tu dors mal* ("when you're tired, you sleep badly"); *à Paris, t'as des métros partout* ("in Paris there are metros everywhere"). It isn't about the listener. It is about as common as *on* for this. People who call each other *vous* use *vous* this way instead.

The production default stays *on*. The examples need the usual orange respellings (*t'è*, *t'a*), and the note should link [3.2](../../../grammaire/03-pronoms.html#s3-2). Evidence: speakers at ease with *tu* used *tu*/*vous* for about 80% of generic reference in Picardy in 1982 (Coveney 2016, Table 3), and 68% of generic reference in French and Swiss family conversations (Waugh & Fonseca-Greber 2002, Table 4).

## (e) *dont* = "whose" and "including" (A10)

**Verdict: "whose" is SOLID.** Two primary studies and the repo's own count agree that it is much commoner in writing. It isn't absent from speech: it is about a quarter of spoken *dont* relatives. **"including" is NOT SOLID as sourced.** Abeillé et al. left verbless *dont* out of both corpora, so they say nothing about it. The only comparison is the repo's own sample of 100 + 100 (11 against 9 cases). It is rarer per word in speech, but as a share of *dont* it is the same in speech and writing.

**What the primary sources say.**

- Abeillé, Anne, Barbara Hemforth & Elodie Winckel (2016). Les relatives en *dont* du français : études empiriques. *CMLF 2016*, SHS Web of Conferences 27: 14011. [doi:10.1051/shsconf/20162714011](https://doi.org/10.1051/shsconf/20162714011). Read in full from an archived copy of the SHS PDF. SHS sits behind a captcha, and the HAL record ([halshs-01428303](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-01428303)) has no file.
  - **Writing:** French Treebank, *Le Monde* 1989–95, about 800,000 words. Table 1 has 384 *dont* relatives with a verb. *dont* goes with a verb 73 times (19%), a noun ("whose") 295 (77%), an adjective 6 and an adjunct 10 (mostly *la manière dont*).
  - **Speech:** CFPP2000, 29 long interviews (37 h 45) with residents of Paris and its suburbs. 134 *dont* in all, 107 relatives with a verb. Table 4 has verb 67 (63%), noun 30 (28%), adjective 3 and adjunct 7 (*la façon dont*).
  - **Left out of both** (notes 6 and 16): verbless *dont* (*23 journalistes ont trouvé la mort, dont 9 en Colombie*, "23 journalists were killed, 9 of them in Colombia"), *dont* not tied to any gap, and unfinished clauses.
  - **Verbs:** *parler* 47 of the 67 verb uses (70%; the text says "plus de 71%"), *se souvenir* 10 (15%). The subject is a clitic pronoun in 65 of 67, never a noun before the verb.
  - **Conclusion:** spoken *dont* differs from written but isn't confined to set phrases. Noun-complement *dont* "restent… courants" (remain common) in speech.
