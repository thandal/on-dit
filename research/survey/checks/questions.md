# Check: question words, *c'est quoi* and questions inside a sentence (chapter 8)

This note checks three proposals from the [survey](../README.md), sections [1](../README.md#1-question-words-and-cest-quoi) and [7](../README.md#7-questions-inside-a-sentence), against the studies they rely on. I read each one in full. For each proposal it gives the verdict, what the primary source says and where, what other studies find, and the change for the grammar.

| Item | Verdict | Key number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (a) 8.3: *c'est quoi ?* as the default "what is it?" | **SOLID** | Family meals in Orléans: 68 *c'est quoi* to 5 *qu'est-ce que c'est*. Paris interviews: 80 to 19. |
| (b) 8.2: *comment* and *pourquoi* usually go first | **SOLID** (*pourquoi*: nearly always; *comment*: usually, but the in-place share is real and growing) | Orléans meals: *comment* first 57 times, in place 5; *pourquoi* never in place. Orléans 2010s, all situations: *comment* first 333 of 472 (71%). |
| (c) 8.4: keep the default, add recognition notes | **SOLID** | After *je sais pas* in Paris interviews: normal order 66, *est-ce que* 6. *je sais pas c'est quoi*: 1 in 144 (Paris), 5 in 98 (Orléans meals), 61 in 159 among teenagers in multiethnic Paris suburbs. |

All the numbers the survey quotes match their primary sources. The corrections are about how the numbers are framed. In-place *comment* is not marginal outside one corpus, and *où est-ce que* inside a sentence is a minority form, not a "common" one.

## (a) *c'est quoi ?* for "what is it?" (8.3)

**Verdict: SOLID.** Two primary studies by the same author, on two corpora from France (Orléans family meals and Paris interviews), point the same way. A transcript count over the CEFC-ORFEO corpus agrees (below). The meal sample is small (73 tokens), but the split is lopsided and the other corpora confirm it.

**What the primary sources say.**

- Lefeuvre, Florence (2020). Les interrogatives partielles dans un corpus de théâtre contemporain. *Langages* 217. [doi:10.3917/lang.217.0023](https://doi.org/10.3917/lang.217.0023); preprint [halshs-03146796](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03146796). Read in full.
  - **Data:** the *Repas* (meals) subcorpus of ESLO2 (Enquêtes SocioLinguistiques à Orléans). These are family meals in Orléans, recorded with no researcher present, around 2012–2014. Lefeuvre gives the size as 22,555 words. That seems small for 699 questions, but the counts below don't depend on it. The speakers are of mixed ages: Thiberge (2020) draws meal examples from both his 15–25 and 35–55 age groups. For comparison she also uses Luca Greco's corpus of calls to the emergency medical line (SAMU), 99 questions.
  - **Table** (preprint p. 3, §1): 699 question-word questions. Question word in place 310 (44%), no verb 207 (30%), question word first in normal order 77 (11%), *est-ce que* 57 (8%), inversion or question word as subject 25 (4%), cleft 23 (3%). Leaving out the questions with no verb, 63% have the word in place (§2.2.2, preprint p. 9).
  - **c'est quoi** (§2.2.1, preprint p. 9): "dans Eslo 2, *c'est quoi* survient à 68 reprises (2 occurrences dans le corpus de Luca Greco) alors que *qu'est-ce que c'est* ne revient que 5 fois (0 occurrence dans le corpus de Luca Greco)." Among in-place questions, "la structure en *c'est* est de loin la plus importante" (*c'est* + question word is by far the commonest type) (§2.2.2). **Matches the survey.**
  - "What" with another verb (§2.2.1): 67 *qu'est-ce que* against 62 in-place *quoi*. **Matches** (8.3 already treats them as equals).
- Lefeuvre, Florence (2015). Analyse grammaticale et sur corpus de l'expression *c'est quoi (?)*. In Dostie & Hadermann (eds), *La dia-variation en français actuel*, Peter Lang, 39–62. Open copy [halshs-01519591](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-01519591). Read in full. The survey doesn't cite it.
  - **Data:** CFPP2000, semi-directed interviews about Paris neighbourhoods (about 579,000 words in 2014), speakers of all ages and backgrounds. Most questions in it are asked by the interviewers. The paper also covers Quebec (CFPQ) and Swiss (OFROM) corpora, which I leave out here.
  - **Numbers** (§2, p. 5 of the HAL PDF): CFPP2000 has 80 *c'est quoi* and 19 *qu'est-ce que c'est*. In Frantext, literature also switched: in 2000–2010 there are 148 *c'est quoi* to 99 *qu'est-ce que c'est*, against 66 to 206 in 1970–1980.
  - **Usage detail:** about 75% of *c'est quoi* questions (Paris and Quebec together) name the thing asked about. Two-thirds of those put it after the question: *c'est quoi, ce bruit ?* ("what's that noise?"). The rest put it first: *Draveil, c'est quoi ?* ("Draveil, what's that?"). The order *\*quoi c'est ?* doesn't exist.

**Other studies.**

- Gardner-Chloros & Secova (2018: §2.1.3) describe *c'est quoi ?* as now "characteristic of the colloquial register", with *qu'est-ce que c'est ?* the more standard form. They give no counts.
- Baunaz & Bonan (2023: 13, note 24) explain a puzzle the survey left open. Quillard's (2000) share of in-place questions is reported as 15.6% (Ledegen 2024) and as 41.6% (Guryev 2017, cited by Lefeuvre 2020). The higher figure counts *c'est* + question word (*c'est quoi ce truc ?*) and in-place clefts, which Quillard listed separately. So in her corpus too, *c'est quoi*-type questions make up a large share of all question-word questions. (Quillard 2000 is a Tours thesis, not available online; known only through these citations.)
- Palasis, Faure & Meunier (in *JFLS* 2024, via the Guryev, Dekhissi & Bonan 2024 editorial) find that in children's speech the fixed form *c'est* strongly favours the question word in place (*c'est qui ?*, *c'est quoi ?*).
- No study I found contradicts it. Coveney's 1982 Picardy data (below) has too few "what is it" questions to say anything.

**Corpus check** (CEFC-ORFEO, France only, 3.2 million words; transcript-only, as the [method](../../README.md#method) asks). This is safe in transcripts because the two forms are different words. *c'est quoi* and *c'était quoi* occur 588 times; *qu'est-ce que c'est* and *qu'est-ce que c'était* 110 times, so 84% are *c'est quoi*. Without the phone calls to the Paris transport help line (83 to 0), it is 82%. Every subcorpus favours *c'est quoi* except storytelling (4 to 13), where *qu'est-ce que c'est* turns up in quoted indignation (*mais qu'est-ce que c'est que cette histoire !*, "what on earth is this business?"). In 40 of the 110 *qu'est-ce que c'est* hits it is followed by *que* + noun (*qu'est-ce que c'est que ce truc ?*, "what's this thing?").

**Change for the grammar.** In 8.3, teach *c'est quoi ?* as the way to ask "what is it?" / "what's X?". Give *c'est quoi, ça ?* ("what's that?"), *c'est quoi, ton numéro ?* ("what's your number?"), *c'était quoi ?* ("what was it?"), and the topic-first order *ce mot, c'est quoi ?* ("this word, what is it?"). Note that it is always *c'est quoi*, never "quoi c'est". For recognition: *qu'est-ce que c'est ?* means the same, is more careful, and is the usual form in indignant *qu'est-ce que c'est que ça ?!* ("what on earth is that?"). Evidence: in family meals in Orléans, *c'est quoi* outnumbers *qu'est-ce que c'est* 68 to 5 (Lefeuvre 2020), and in Paris interviews 80 to 19 (Lefeuvre 2015). In the grammar's notation *c'est* is written *c'è* (orange).

## (b) *comment* and *pourquoi* usually go first (8.2)

**Verdict: SOLID.** For *pourquoi* it is close to categorical in every study. For *comment*, putting it first is clearly the usual choice in every corpus from France. The size of the in-place minority varies, though: 8% in Lefeuvre's meals, 29% across ESLO2 in the 2010s, up from 4% in the 1970s. The note should say "usually", not "always".

**What the primary source says.** Lefeuvre (2020), §2.1.2, preprint pp. 7–8: "Pour le sous-corpus Repas (Eslo 2), nous n'avons relevé aucun emploi de *pourquoi* in situ, 57 emplois de *comment* en position frontale et 5 en position in situ." The same paragraph adds a counter-example the survey left out. In Greco's emergency-call corpus, *comment* is in place 5 times and first 4 times, so 9 tokens. Lefeuvre doesn't give the total for *pourquoi* or say which structures the 57 include. They probably include *comment veux-tu…* with inversion, which she counts 5 times. **Matches the survey** (57 in 62; *pourquoi* never in place).

**Other studies.**

- **Baunaz & Bonan (2023)**. Activation levels: a fresh perspective on French wh in-situ. *Isogloss* 9(1)/7. [doi:10.5565/rev/isogloss.305](https://doi.org/10.5565/rev/isogloss.305), [PDF](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/isogloss/isogloss_a2023v9n1/isogloss_a2023v9n1a02.pdf). Read in full. They take every direct, information-seeking question with *comment, où, quand, qui, quoi* in the whole of ESLO (Orléans, all situations), checked against the audio. They leave out clefts, *c'est qui ?*, echo questions and *pourquoi*. Table 7 (p. 25) shows *comment* first in 539 of 562 (96%) in ESLO1 (1968–74) and in 333 of 472 (71%) in ESLO2 (2010s). *où* ("where") went the other way: in place in 209 of 293 (71%) in ESLO2. So *comment* still goes first in the 2010s, but its in-place share rose from 4% to 29%.
- **Coveney, Aidan (1989)**. *Variability in interrogation and negation in spoken French*. PhD thesis, Newcastle. [PDF](https://dspace.ncl.ac.uk/jspui/bitstream/10443/191/1/coveney89.pdf). This is the data behind Coveney 1996/2002. Read in full. It covers 30 holiday-camp workers aged 17–37, interviewed in the Somme (Picardy) in 1982, with 122 question-word questions in all. Table 6.13 (p. 311) shows the question word at the end with *comment* 1 time in 9 and *pourquoi* 0 in 1; with *combien* ("how much") it is 7 in 7 and with *où* 1 in 10. Coveney cites Behnstedt (1973) for the same pattern: *comment* and *pourquoi* disfavour the end position, while *quand* and *combien* favour it. A tiny sample, but it agrees.
- **Gardner-Chloros & Secova (2018: note 7)**: in their Paris-suburb teenagers' data, "*Pourquoi* is the only question word which never occurred in situ".
- Not read, only cited: the editorial by Guryev, Dekhissi & Bonan (2024: 7–8) cites Quillard (2000: 123), Lefeuvre & Rossi-Gensane (2015: 13) and Adli (2015: 192) for *pourquoi* rarely in place, and Coveney (2002: 221) and Dekhissi (2013) for *comment* favouring first position. It also notes that in-place *pourquoi* is not impossible, e.g. *Vous les aimiez pourquoi ?* ("Why did you like them?", Sartre). Myers & Pellet (2014), on *pourquoi* in casual conversation, were behind a paywall.

**Corpus check** (CEFC-ORFEO, France; transcript-only, automatic with spot checks, rough).

- *pourquoi* + subject at the start of an utterance: 424 hits. *pourquoi* at the end after a subject and verb: 25 hits, but only 2 or 3 of those are real direct questions (*tu attends pourquoi ?*, "what are you waiting for?", in a told story). So *pourquoi* comes first well over 95% of the time.
- *comment* + subject at the start: 806 hits (plus 24 with inversion). *comment* at the end: 60. In a spot check of 40 of each, about 70% were real direct questions; the rest were fillers like *comment je veux dire*, *comment ça ?* used for "what do you mean?", and indirect questions. That gives roughly 580 first to 40 in place, about 93% first.
- The in-place cases cluster on a few verbs. *ça s'écrit comment ?* ("how is it spelled?") is the only frame where in place wins, 6 to 2. *ça se passe comment ?* is 12 to 85, *on fait comment ?* 1 to 29, *tu fais comment ?* 2 to 15, *comment ça se fait ?* ("how come?") 35 to 1.

**Change for the grammar.** In 8.2, after "you can also put it first", say that *pourquoi* and *comment* usually go first: *pourquoi tu ris ?* ("why are you laughing?"), *comment tu fais ?* ("how do you do it?"), *comment ça s'est passé ?* ("how did it go?"). For recognition, *comment* at the end is also heard, especially in short set questions: *ça s'écrit comment ?* ("how is it spelled?"), *ça se passe comment ?* ("how does it work / how's it going?"), *il s'appelle comment ?* ("what's he called?"). The table's model for *comment* would be better as *comment tu fais ?* (or *comment ça s'écrit ?*). *ça s'écrit comment ?* can stay as the example of in-place *comment* that people do say. The practice that asks for "the question word at the end" uses neither word, so it stands. Evidence: in Orléans family meals *comment* comes first 57 times out of 62 and *pourquoi* never stays in place (Lefeuvre 2020). Across Orléans recordings of the 2010s, *comment* comes first 71% of the time (Baunaz & Bonan 2023).

## (c) Questions inside a sentence (8.4)

**Verdict: SOLID**: keep normal order as the default and add two recognition notes. The figures come from four primary studies, all read, three of them on data from France (Paris interviews, Orléans meals, Paris-suburb teenagers), and they agree. One wording in the survey needs toning down. The *est-ce que* type is a minority form (2–8% overall). It is socially unremarkable, but not "common".

**What the primary sources say.**

- **Branca-Rosoff, Sonia & Florence Lefeuvre (2016).** Le CFPP2000 : constitution, outils et analyses. Le cas des interrogatives indirectes. *Corpus* 15: 265–284. [doi:10.4000/corpus.3043](https://doi.org/10.4000/corpus.3043), open copy [halshs-01520128](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-01520128). Read in full.
  - **Data:** CFPP2000 in March 2015, 602,000 words, 42.5 hours, 32 interviews in central and eastern Paris and a few nearby suburbs.
  - **Tableau 1** (§3.2): after *je (ne) sais pas* ("I don't know"), normal order beats *est-ce que* 66 to 6. By question word: *qui* 4–1, *où* 7–3, *pourquoi* 8–0, *comment* 23–0, *ce que / qu'est-ce que* 24–2, *quand* 0–0. "Où favorise l'emploi de *est-ce que*. *Comment* et *pourquoi* l'inhibent."
  - There are 27 indirect questions with *est-ce que* in the whole corpus, used by a 23-year-old medical student from the 7th arrondissement (*je sais pas où est-ce que je vais*, "I don't know where I'm going") and a primary-school teacher. Speakers don't correct themselves.
  - In-place forms (§3.5): only 2, both from young men from Saint-Ouen.
  - **Matches the survey.**
- **Lefeuvre, Florence & Nathalie Rossi-Gensane (2017).** Les interrogatives indirectes en discours informel oral. *Langue française* 196: 51–74. [doi:10.3917/lf.196.0051](https://doi.org/10.3917/lf.196.0051), open copy [halshs-03360558](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03360558). Read in full. The survey had it only through Ledegen.
  - **Tableau 1** (p. 64): all of CFPP2000 (685,000 words, 2017), indirect questions after *savoir*. Of 144 with a finite verb, 120 are normal order, 7 have the noun subject after the verb, 14 use *est-ce que*, 2 use *comment que*, and 1 has the question word in place (0.7%).
  - **Tableau 2** (p. 65): the ESLO2 meals. Of 98, 87 are normal order, 2 inverted, 2 *est-ce que*, 2 *qu- que*, and 5 in place (5%): *je sais pas c'est quoi*, *je sais pas c'est quand le jour*, *tu sais pas c'est qui*.
  - The authors put the in-place forms down to the informal setting and the speakers' youth ("le plus âgé a 29 ans", the oldest is 29). There are none in ESLO1 (1968–71).
  - Standard order is "autour de 90 %" (about 90%) in both corpora (§5).
  - **Matches the survey** (1 in 144, 5 in 98).
- **Gardner-Chloros, Penelope & Maria Secova (2018).** Grammatical change in Paris French: in situ question words in embedded contexts. *JFLS* 28(2): 181–207. [doi:10.1017/S0959269518000091](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269518000091), [accepted manuscript](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/22079/). Read in full.
  - **Data:** Multicultural Paris French (2010–2014), 34 recordings, 341,400 words, 77 speakers aged 10–19 in the Paris suburbs, recorded among peers.
  - **Tables 4.1–4.2:** 61 of 159 indirect questions have the word in place (38%): *je sais pas c'est quoi* 30, *c'est qui / comment / combien* 31. Seven *qu'est-ce que* tokens are excluded.
  - **Table 5:** in place 57% for active bilinguals (48/84), 27% passive bilinguals, 7.9% monolingual French speakers (3/38).
  - **Table 6:** 7% for speakers with two French parents.
  - Also more frequent among boys (52% against 24%), speakers with mixed friendship groups, and 10–14-year-olds.
  - **Matches the survey**, which should say "teenagers" rather than "young people".
- **Ledegen, Gudrun (2024).** The embedded Wh- in situ clause: French tout court? *JFLS* 34: 39–71. [doi:10.1017/S095926952400005X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095926952400005X). Read in full. It is a review plus Quebec data. For France it reports Marchessou (2018) on the Strasbourg suburbs (35 in 63, 55%). It adds examples from a France Info interviewee, doctoral and master's students in Brittany, and French rap. Its France numbers are the ones above.

**Corpus check** (CEFC-ORFEO, France; transcript-only).

- After *sais/savez pas* or *sais plus*, *c'est/c'était quoi* occurs 6 times against 55 for *ce que c'est/c'était*, about 10% for this phrase. Five of the six speakers are students or graduates.
- Inside a sentence, *où est-ce que* occurs 5 times against 76 for *où* + subject. Across all question words, *est-ce que* occurs 28 times against about 810 for the plain form (about 3%).

**Change for the grammar.** Keep 8.4 as it is. Add a "you'll hear" note:

- *je sais pas où est-ce qu'il habite* ("I don't know where he lives") is heard from all kinds of speakers, mostly with *où*. It is a minority form; the order taught here is far more usual.
- *je sais pas c'est quoi* ("I don't know what it is") and *je sais pas c'est qui* ("…who it is") are heard mainly from young people. Among teenagers in the multiethnic suburbs of Paris they are close to the norm. Many people frown on them.

Evidence: in Paris interviews, normal order beats *est-ce que* 66 to 6 after *je sais pas* (Branca-Rosoff & Lefeuvre 2016). In-place forms are 1 in 144 there and 5 in 98 in Orléans meals (Lefeuvre & Rossi-Gensane 2017), but 38% among Paris-suburb teenagers (Gardner-Chloros & Secova 2018).

## Context: how often the question word stays in place (8.2's default)

This isn't one of the three proposals, but it frames them. In Orléans meals the share of in-place questions is high: 63% of questions with a verb (Lefeuvre 2020) and 67 of 92 (73%) in the meal recordings of ESLO1 and ESLO2 together (Thiberge 2020, *Acquisition et maîtrise des interrogatives partielles en français*, thesis, Université Paris Cité, [tel-03784454](https://shs.hal.science/tel-03784454), Tableau 4.12). It is much lower in interviews, about a third (same table). ESLO as a whole went from 24% in place in the 1960s–70s to 58% in the 2010s (Thiberge, Tableau 4.9). One study disagrees. Gotowski & Déprez (2020, conference abstract on ESLO) reportedly find 30% in place at meals and 12% in interviews. I could only read a summary of it, and their coding probably left out *c'est quoi*-type questions. Coveney's 1982 interviews had 16% (Table 4.6). All the studies agree that the share depends on the situation and has risen over time. Both points fit item (a): much of the in-place share is *c'est* + question word.
