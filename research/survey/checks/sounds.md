# Check: *faut* with no *il*, and the ties after *est* (chapters 1 and 5)

This note checks two proposals from the [survey](../README.md), sections [2](../README.md#2-faut-with-no-il) and [3](../README.md#3-linking-after-est-and-other-optional-ties), against the studies they rely on. For each it gives the verdict, what the primary source says and where, what other studies find, and the change for the grammar. Every source marked "read" was read in full or through its methods and results sections; the few known only through another author are marked as such.

| Item | Verdict | Key number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (a) 1.7: *faut* with no *il* as a relaxed form | **SOLID** (the change; the survey's numbers need correcting) | Orléans, 5,925 cases of *faut*: *il* left out 35% of the time in 1968–71, 57% in 2008. Main clauses in 2008: 64%. Meals among family and friends in 2008: 72%. |
| (b) 1.6, 5.4, être list: drop the ties after *est* | **NOT SOLID** for *est* before a participle; holds for *la porte est‿ouverte* | The 44% is real, but it counts *est* before any word. Before a participle the studies find 70–79%: *est allé(e)* 26 of 33 in PFC conversations from France. |
| (b) the other optional ties | **Keep all** | *très* + adjective 95–97%, *plus* + adjective 94%, *quand* 78%, *chez* + pronoun always, adjective + noun always. |

## (a) *faut* with no *il* (1.7)

**Verdict: SOLID.** The primary study is Widera's own full analysis of the Orléans corpus (her 2024 thesis), which supersedes the 2022 article the survey cites. It is France, mostly sociolinguistic interviews plus family meals, 5,925 tokens, with the transcription checked against the audio. Older French corpora, a transcript count over CEFC-ORFEO and an acceptability study all point the same way. The survey's specific numbers don't match the full analysis (see below), but the corrected numbers make the case for the change stronger, not weaker.

**What the primary source says.**

- Widera, Carmen (2024). *On the Syntax of Impersonal Constructions in French and Portuguese: the Use of Expletive Pronouns in Null and Non Null Subject Languages*. Doctoral thesis, Universität Konstanz. [KOPS](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/71427), [PDF](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/bitstreams/3d59b3ac-8f8e-418a-a388-5fb76e9c4415/download). Chapter 4.1 (corpus study) and 4.3 (acceptability study) read in full. She says (p. 65) that the 2022 *Langages* article reported "first results of this analysis based on a part of the corpus".
  - **Data** (pp. 67–72): every present-tense *faut* in ESLO (Enquêtes SocioLinguistiques à Orléans), ESLO1 (1968–71) and ESLO2 (2008 on). Mostly sociolinguistic interviews, plus family meals and conferences. Interviewers' speech removed; *faut* after a word ending in an "i" sound (*oui faut*) removed as undecidable; repeated *faut* in one sentence counted once. N = 5,925. The ESLO transcription guide tells transcribers not to restore an *il* that wasn't said, and Widera checked the transcription against the audio (p. 69).
  - **Overall** (Figure 4.1, p. 74): *il* left out 1,291 of 3,723 times in ESLO1 (35%) and 1,258 of 2,202 in ESLO2 (57%); 43% overall.
  - **Clause type** (Figure 4.2, p. 76, declaratives only): main clauses 40.8% (1968–71) → 64.1% (2008), 49.8% pooled; clauses after a conjunction (*que*, *si*…) 16.0% → 27.3%, 19.4% pooled; relative clauses 6.3% → 11.4%, 7.9% pooled; *comme il faut* ("properly") 0 of 52.
  - **Negation** (pp. 77–78): with *ne … pas*, *il* is always said. With *pas* alone it is left out 64% (1968–71) and 81% (2008) of the time, so *faut pas* ("you mustn't") is the usual way to say it. Without negation: 32% → 55%.
  - **Age** (Figure 4.5, p. 80; groups 15–34, 35–55, over 55): 1968–71: 35%, 31%, 40%. 2008: 68%, 52%, 51%. The youngest group differs significantly from the other two.
  - **Education** (Figure 4.7, p. 82): in 2008, 62% (basic), 63% (secondary), 54% (degree). Over half in every group, slightly less among graduates.
  - **Setting** (Figure 4.8, p. 83): in 2008, conferences 10 of 31 (32%), interviews 897 of 1,574 (57%), meals 181 of 252 (72%). The more informal, the less *il*.
  - **Only before a consonant** (pp. 45–47): leaving out *il* before a vowel (*a fallu que…* for *il a fallu que…*, "we had to") occurs in ESLO but is "extremely rare".
  - **Other verbs** (acceptability study, 2018, 389 native speakers who grew up in France, spoken stimuli in an informal setting; pp. 111, 124): without *il*, *faut* (z = 0.46) and *vaut mieux* (0.45) are rated almost as natural as with it; *suffit* (0.08) and *paraît* (0.07) above average; *s'agit* (0.00) average; *reste*, *fait beau/froid* and *est possible/probable* below. The corpus study covers *faut* only, because the other verbs were too rare in ESLO.

**The survey's numbers.** They come from Leis (2024: §3.2), [doi:10.1017/S0959269524000127](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269524000127), open access, which I read. Leis reports Widera 2022 correctly: 57% in main clauses, 21% in subordinate clauses and 8% in relative clauses (Widera 2022: 61), and young speakers 16% → 66% (Widera 2022: 63). Widera 2022 itself is behind Cairn and wasn't reachable. The thesis, on the whole corpus, gives different figures: main clauses 50% pooled (41% → 64%), young speakers 35% → 68%. The survey's "57%" matches the thesis's overall 2008 rate, not a main-clause rate. Use the thesis numbers.

**Other studies.**

- Zimmermann & Kaiser (2014), three French corpora of the 1970s–80s (as summarised by Widera 2024: 41; not read): *il* left out with *falloir* in 21.6%, 20.0% and 47.2% of main clauses; in embedded clauses 0–12.5% on very few tokens.
- Culbertson & Legendre (2014), acceptability study with 24 speakers from France (via Widera 2024: 34–37; not read): leaving out *il* is most acceptable with frequent "modal" verbs, above all *falloir*; least with weather verbs.
- Outside France, for comparison only: Swiss conversation, *il* absent with *falloir* 44% (Fonseca-Greber 2004); Montreal, 46% (1971) → 66% (2012–13), main clauses 75% (Djuikui Dountsop, Auger & Tremblay 2020); Swiss text messages 48% for all impersonal verbs (Stark 2013). All via Widera 2024: 38–44.
- **Corpus check** (CEFC-ORFEO, France only; transcript-only, as the [method](../../README.md#method) asks). Every *faut* with no *il* (or *i*) before it, skipping *ne* and object pronouns; repeats and *ça faut* left out; "subordinate" means *que, qui, si, quand, comme, parce que, où…* comes just before. Main clauses: 1,700 of 5,459 (31%) without *il*; subordinate: 86 of 1,016 (8.5%). By subcorpus (main clauses): everyday interaction (CLAPI) 55%, interviews (TUFS) 44%, work meetings 43%, Paris interviews (CFPP2000) 39%, TCOF 24%, CRFP 22%, calls to the Paris transport help line 9%, storytelling 3%. *fallait*, *faudrait* and *faudra* (all clauses): 18%, 28%, 32%. These are lower than Orléans, which fits transcribers sometimes writing *il faut* for what was said, but the same clause asymmetry and the same pull of informal settings show up.
- The *Encyclopédie grammaticale du français* (encyclogram.fr) wasn't reachable (its certificate doesn't match the host). No other study of France with rates turned up; Ashby, Laks and Armstrong study the l of *il* (*i faut*), not its absence.

**Change for the grammar.**

- **1.7:** add a row to the relaxed forms, like *y a*: *{faut|il faut}*, "you have to, we have to, it's necessary". Example: *faut y aller* ("we've got to go"), *faut pas toucher* ("don't touch", literally "mustn't touch"), *faut que je parte* ("I've got to go").
- Say when the *il* stays: usually after *que*, *qui*, *si*, *quand* and the like (*je crois qu'il faut partir*, "I think we have to leave"; in 2008 it was left out about 1 time in 4 after *que* or *si*, 1 in 9 after *qui*), always in *comme il faut* ("properly"), always with *ne* (*il ne faut pas*), and before a vowel (*il a fallu*, "we had to"). Never with weather: *il pleut* ("it's raining"), *il fait beau* ("it's nice out").
- For recognition: *vaut mieux* ("better", *vaut mieux pas*, "better not") drops *il* just as easily; *paraît que* ("apparently") and *suffit de* ("all you need is") sometimes do; *fallait*, *faudrait* also turn up without *il*.
- The bullet "*il faut* is often 'i faut'" needs another example (*il veut* is often "i veut").
- Evidence: in Orléans in 2008, *il* was left out in 64% of main-clause *faut*, 72% at family meals and 68% among speakers under 35, up from 35% overall in 1968–71 (Widera 2024, 5,925 tokens); speakers across France rate *faut* without *il* almost as natural as with it.

## (b) Ties after *est*, and the other optional ties (1.6, 5.3, 5.4, 5.7, être list)

**Verdict: NOT SOLID** for dropping the tie in *il est‿allé*, *il est‿arrivé*, *il est‿entré* and *elle est‿arrivée*. The survey's number matches its source, but it measures the wrong thing for these ties. Mallet's 44% counts *est* before every kind of word (*est un*, *est à*, *est en*, *est* + adjective, *est* + participle), from speakers 27% of whom are outside France. The studies that separate out *est* before a past participle find it linked 70–79% of the time, on small samples. The drop **does hold** for *la porte est‿ouverte*: *est* there is a linking verb, not a helper, and no study shows that link to be common.

**What the primary source says.**

- Mallet, Géraldine-M. (2008). *La liaison en français : descriptions et analyses dans le corpus PFC*. Thèse de doctorat, Université Paris Ouest Nanterre La Défense. [PDF](https://www.projet-pfc.net/wp-content/uploads/2009/02/these_mallet.pdf). Chapters 5.2 and 6 read.
  - **Data:** 24 PFC (Phonologie du Français Contemporain) surveys online in September 2007, 259 speakers (Figure 5-11, p. 171): 188 from France (66 south, 122 north) and 71 (27%) from Abidjan, Gembloux, Liège, Tournai, Nyon and Réunion. Recorded early 2000s. Speakers under 30: 94; over 60: 70 (Figure 5-13).
  - **Conversation only:** the per-word tables in 6.3 are built from the guided and free conversations, 28,519 coded sites (p. 186; "les liaisons réalisées dans les conversations libre et guidée, soit un total de 12 841", p. 211). Reading the PFC text is kept separate: 45% linked in conversation against 63% in reading (Figure 6-6, p. 189).
  - **Per word** (Figures 6-47 and 6-48, pp. 229–231; 6-66 and 6-77, pp. 243 and 251): *est* 279 of 636 (43.9%), *c'est* 413 of 1,470 (28.1%), *sont* 19%, *était* 8.5%, *ont* 9.6%, *suis* 13.5%; *très* 140 of 145 (96.6%), *dans* 95%, *chez* 82.8%, *quand* 498 of 639 (77.9%), *tout* 82.9%, *plus* 159 of 248 (64.1%), *petit* 36 of 48. All match the survey's table.
  - **Not split by what follows.** Footnote 119 (p. 229) only says *est* is always the verb. The 636 cases mix every context. Mallet notes the same problem for *tout* (low because of *pas du tout*, "not at all") and *petit*/*grand*, which link "categorically" when the adjective comes before a noun (*petit ami*) and never when it follows one or is a noun (p. 240).
  - **Age** (Figure 6-10, p. 194): free conversation, under 30 41.7%, over 60 50%.

**Other studies.**

- Durand, Laks, Calderone & Tchobanov (2011). Que savons-nous de la liaison aujourd'hui ? *Langue française* 169: 103–135. [halshs-00990563](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-00990563) (record only); full text [copy](https://seai.web.uniroma1.it/sites/default/files/Durand%20et%20alii%20liaison.pdf). Read.
  - **est allé(e)** (Table 8, pp. 129–130; data from Coquillon et al. 2010): PFC conversations, 67 speakers from the north and 67 from the south of France. With liaison: south 15 of 15, north 11 of 18 (61%); together 26 of 33 (79%). For comparison *suis allé(e)* 15 of 111 (14%). The survey quotes these numbers but doesn't draw the conclusion: they contradict using 44% for *est* + participle.
  - **chez** (p. 117): "chez fait liaison catégorique avec un post-clitique : che[z] eux, che[z] elle" (*chez* always links before a pronoun). Before a noun: south 9 of 11, north 22 of 27 (p. 130).
  - **Adjective + noun** (p. 116): not quite as regular as phonologists claim; a few non-links (*petit // accent*).
  - **Overall:** 372 speakers, 35 surveys: 43.4% linked in conversation, 59.4% in reading (p. 113). Younger speakers link less: 38.7% (under 20) to 48.0% (over 60) in conversation (Table 5, p. 127).
  - *est un* / *est une* (Table 3, p. 123): 53 linked, at least 65 not.
- Ranson, Diana L. (2008). La liaison variable dans un corpus du français méridional. *CMLF 2008*. [doi:10.1051/cmlf08279](https://doi.org/10.1051/cmlf08279), [archived PDF](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://www.linguistiquefrancaise.org/articles/cmlf/pdf/2008/01/cmlf08279.pdf). Read. 16 speakers from the south of France in conversation with the author, 2005–06 (Table 1). Table 2 (p. 1673): *être* as helper (*il est arrivé*) 33 of 45 (73%); *être* as linking verb (*c'est une langue*) 98 of 377 (26%); *quand* 36 of 52 (69%); *très*, *plus* 49 of 51 (96%); prepositions 88 of 93 (95%). The same table gives Malécot's (1975) Paris figures for educated speakers in conversation, early 1970s: helper *être* 30 of 43 (70%), linking *être* 72% (Malécot not read).
- Booij, Geert & Daan de Jong (1987). The domain of liaison: theories and data. *Linguistics* 25: 1005–1025. [PDF](https://geertbooij.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/02/booij-1987-de-jong-liaison.pdf). Read. Tours, 38 speakers in interviews (De Jong et al. 1981): *est* 43% (N = 213), *sont* 20%, *suis* 19% (Table 7, p. 1016); by age, *est* 33% (youngest), 35%, 67% (oldest) (Table 9, p. 1017). Radio in 1961–62 (Ågren 1973): *est* 97% of 2,669 (p. 1013). *est* is not split by what follows.
- Baude, Olivier & Céline Dugua (2011). (Re)faire le corpus d'Orléans quarante ans après. *Corpus* 10: 99–118. [doi:10.4000/corpus.2036](https://doi.org/10.4000/corpus.2036). Read §58–61. Orléans 1968–71 (De Jong 1994, not read): *est* + any word 69%, *sont* 46%, *quand* 96.3%, *ont* 8.7%. In their own 11-hour sample, *est*/*sont* 64%, from 36% to 100% by speaker; one speaker drops from 100% in the interview to 19% in informal talk afterwards.
- Barreca, Giulia (2014). Multidimensionnalité de la liaison variable et difficultés de classification. *JEP 2014*. [halshs-01078035](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-01078035). Read. Adverb + adjective in PFC conversations and Paris interviews (CFPP2000), 505 cases (Table 2): *très* 95% (n = 198), *plus* 94% (n = 126), *moins* 65%, *trop* 59%, *pas* 0%.
- Adda-Decker et al. (2012), [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/F12-1069.pdf), read: 46 young Parisian students talking with friends, automatic alignment; "optional" sites linked 11% of the time, but their optional sites include many the PFC wouldn't code, and they give no per-word rates.
- Eychenne, Lyche, Durand & Coquillon (2014), PFC (book chapter, not read; as cited by [Racine & Detey 2016](https://libra.unine.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/04a5cf38-cb48-4281-9775-8f6f84dbb61a/content): 9, 12): *en* 99.35%, *sans* 93.13%, *chez* 75.68% (always before a pronoun, about half before a noun); *(c')est* 31.68% in conversation against 76.92% in reading.

**Taken together.** After *est* in general, conversation in France links a bit under half the time today (PFC 44%, Tours 1970s 43%), less among young speakers. But the helper *est* before a participle links much more often than the linking-verb *est*: 73% against 26% in one corpus, 79% (*est allé*) in the PFC, 70% in 1970s Paris. The samples are small (33–45 tokens each) and the northern PFC figure is lower (11 of 18), so the link is clearly optional, but it is the more common pronunciation.

The verb forms the survey lists as rarely linked (*c'est* 28%, *sont* 19%, *suis* 13.5%, *ont* 10%, *était* 8.5%) aren't tied anywhere in the grammar, so nothing changes there.

**What the evidence supports** (not a SOLID change, but what the main session could do):

- **5.3:** write *la porte {è|est} ouverte* with no tie.
- **être list, 5.4, 5.7:** keep *il est‿allé*, *est‿arrivé*, *est‿entré*, *elle est‿arrivée*. If the grammar wants ties to mean "nearly always", dropping them is defensible too, but the reason would be that the link is optional, not that it is rare.
- **1.6:** "The tie is always shown, so you never have to guess" can stay, with one sentence added: a few ties mark links most people make but can skip in fast speech, after *est* before a participle (*il est‿allé*) and after *quand*; after other verb forms (*c'est*, *sont*, *suis*, *ont*, *était*) linking is rare in conversation and isn't shown. The survey's proposed wording ("after verbs (*est*…) linking is optional and usually skipped") would be wrong for *est* + participle.
- **Survey section 3:** correct "after *est*, conversation makes the liaison less than half the time" to apply to *est* in general, and add the participle figures.
