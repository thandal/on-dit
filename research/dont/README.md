# dont in speech

**Question.** Section 10.4 teaches *qui, que, où* and *dont* as the four relative pronouns, with *dont* for verbs and expressions that take *de*: *le mec dont je t'ai parlé* ("the guy I told you about"), *le truc dont j'ai besoin* ("the thing I need"). Spoken French is often said to avoid *dont*, using *que* instead (*le truc que j'ai besoin*) or *que* plus a pronoun (*le truc que j'en ai besoin*). How much do speakers in France use *dont*, and what do they say instead?

**Answer.** *dont* is rare in speech overall, but not in the job 10.4 gives it.

- Overall, *dont* is 1–2% of relative pronouns in recorded conversation (1.1% in ESLO2, 1% in Larrivée's conversations, 1.6% in the corpus check), against 11–18% in edited writing, and about six times rarer per word.
- Most of that gap is *dont* meaning "whose" (*un auteur dont le livre…*, "an author whose book…"), *dont* meaning "including" (*dix blessés, dont deux graves*, "ten injured, two of them seriously") and formal verbs (*disposer de*, *être issu de*). Speech uses these much less.
- With the everyday verbs 10.4 is about (*parler de, avoir besoin de, se souvenir de, s'occuper de, faire partie de*), the corpus check finds speech using *dont* about as often per word as writing. It's the usual choice: about four in five such relative clauses have *dont*, most of them *dont je parle* or *dont on a parlé* ("that I'm talking about", "that we talked about").
- The main alternative is plain *que* with no *de* (*ce que t'as besoin*, "what you need"). No published study counts it. In the corpus check it's about one case in five or six, said by educated adults in relaxed talk. It's commonest with *avoir besoin* and *avoir envie* ("feel like") and rare with *parler*.
- The *que + en* type (*le truc que j'en ai besoin*), which linguists call *décumul*, is close to absent in every corpus studied.
- *la façon dont* and *la manière dont* ("the way that") are frequent, fixed phrases in speech: about one spoken *dont* in seven.

**For the grammar.** 10.4 is right to teach *dont* for use, and *parler de* and *avoir besoin de* are the right examples. Three changes:

1. Add *la façon dont* as a phrase to learn, in 10.4: *j'aime pas la façon dont il parle* ("I don't like the way he talks").
2. Add a "you'll hear" line to 10.4, for recognition only: in relaxed speech, *que* without *de*, as in *ce que t'as besoin* or *c'est le mec que je t'ai parlé*. Speakers also often avoid the question altogether: *ce que t'as envie de faire* ("what you feel like doing") uses plain *que*, because *que* is the object of *faire*. Don't mention *que j'en ai besoin*; learners won't hear it.
3. In the appendix (A10), add *dont* = "whose" (*des gens dont les parents…*, "people whose parents…") and *dont* = "including" (*dont deux enfants*, "including two children"). Both are mainly written.

## What the research says

### dont in speech: rare, and concentrated on a few verbs

- **Blanche-Benveniste** called *dont* relatives rare in spoken corpora, and said they mostly depend on a few verbs (1995, as summarized by Abeillé et al. 2016). With Martin (2010), she counted *dont* in CORPAIX, 2 million words of spoken French from Aix-en-Provence. Their finding: *dont* is little used in conversation, and when it is, it's in formulaic phrases built on a few words. Of its occurrences:
  - 49% depend on a verb. *parler* is about half of those, followed by *avoir besoin de* and *se passer de* ("do without").
  - 25% depend on a noun, very often a family word (*dont la mère*, "whose mother").
  - 15% are *la façon dont* or *la manière dont*.
  - 11% come with hesitation, or alternate with *que*.

  These figures are as reported by Péclard (2022). I couldn't consult the book.
- **ESLO2** (Orléans, about 900,000 words; Larrivée & Skrovec 2016, figures in Larrivée 2018) has 7,623 *qui*, 2,373 *que*, 841 *où*, 124 *dont* and 28 forms of *lequel*. That makes *dont* 1.1% of relative pronouns, about 140 per million words. In free conversation, native speakers used 3 *dont* in 299 relatives (Larrivée 2018).
- **Abeillé, Hemforth & Winckel (2016)** compared 107 *dont* relatives in CFPP2000 (Paris interviews) with 384 in the French Treebank (newspaper text):
  - In speech, 63% of *dont* depend on a verb, against 19% in the newspaper.
  - The verb is *parler* 71% of the time and *se souvenir* 15% (as reported by Péclard 2022).
  - The subject is almost always a pronoun: *dont elle me parle* ("that she tells me about"), not *dont ma mère me parle*.
  - *dont* depending on a noun ("whose") is 27% in speech and 77% in the newspaper.

  They conclude that speech uses *dont* differently from writing but, against Blanche-Benveniste, not only in fixed phrases.
- **The alternatives with a preposition** are rarer still. CFPP2000 has 11 *de qui*, only 3 of them relatives, and even in literary texts *de qui* is about 60 times rarer than *dont* (Abeillé & Winckel 2020). *lequel* and its forms are three times as common in writing as in speech (Bilger & Cappeau 2013).

### The non-standard relatives

- **Guiraud (1966)** set out four variants of *l'homme dont je vous parle* ("the man I'm telling you about"):
  1. *dont je vous parle*
  2. *que je vous parle*
  3. *que je vous en parle*
  4. *dont je vous en parle*

  He presented them as *français populaire* ("working-class French") simplifying the relative system.
- **Gadet (1995; 1997)** named the same types:
  - standard: *l'homme dont je parle*
  - *défective*: *l'homme que je parle*
  - *résomptive*, with a pronoun taking up the noun: *l'homme que je parle de lui*
  - *pléonastique*, marked twice: *l'homme dont je parle de lui*

  In the resumptive type, the relative pronoun's jobs are split up (*décumul*). A "passe-partout" *que* marks the clause as subordinate, and a pronoun, a possessive or a stranded preposition shows the noun's role. She treats the double-marked type as a cross between standard and non-standard, not a type of its own (Gadet as quoted in Péclard 2022). Blanche-Benveniste (1990) puts the difference in prepositions: non-standard usage spreads *que* to where the norm has preposition + pronoun. The *Grammaire méthodique du français* (Riegel, Pellat & Rioul) files all of these under "langue parlée populaire" (as reported by Péclard 2022).
- **How common the resumptive type is.** The corpus studies all find it rare:
  - Larrivée & Skrovec found *la femme que j't'en ai parlé*-type relatives so rare that ESLO2 has none (Larrivée 2018).
  - Su & Donati (2023) found a resumptive pronoun in 85 of 1,713 *que* relatives (5%) in ESLO interviews from 1968–71, and 36 of 1,132 (3%) from 2008 on. Most were for subjects and objects. Only 4 and 5 were for a verb's complement with a preposition, like *une réforme de l'orthographe qu'on en a parlé* ("a spelling reform we talked about").
  - Rouget & Salze (1985) also report them rare. Péclard (2022) couldn't find enough in the PFC corpus to build teaching material. The OFROM FAQ (Switzerland) says relatives like *le livre que je t'ai parlé* are "en réalité assez rares".
- **The gap.** I found no count of the *défective* type (*que* with no *de* and no pronoun) against *dont* in France. The corpus check below fills that.

### Who uses which

- The descriptive tradition ties the non-standard types to *français populaire* (Guiraud 1966; Gadet 1995, 1997; Riegel et al.). Larrivée & Skrovec (2019) find instead that the type of interaction matters most: the more prepared and formal the exchange, the more standard the relatives. They see the vernacular and the standard as two ends of a continuum, not two systems.
- **Canada; may not carry over to France.** Lefebvre & Fournier (1978) described relatives in Montreal French. In a study of working-class Montreal teenagers (Lefebvre et al. 1982, as summarized by Connors & Reighard 1984), the speakers understood standard forms and could produce most of them on demand. Relative clauses were the one area where they hadn't mastered the standard.

### Learners and teaching

- **Textbooks.** Péclard (2022) went through four recent French-as-a-foreign-language textbook series. All of them teach *dont* as "the relative for a complement with *de*" from A2 on. None gives frequencies, separates speech from writing, or points out the phrases *dont je parle* and *dont j'ai besoin*. The non-standard forms appear once, in *Cosmopolite* A2, as errors in the mouth of a stereotyped "jeune de banlieue" ("kid from the projects").
- **Production or recognition.** Reviewing Ball's *Colloquial French Grammar*, Gadet (2002) accepts asking learners for the standard equivalent of a resumptive relative, "but why have them PRODUCE them?"
- **Advanced learners in conversation** (Norwegian students in France) used relatives in the same proportions as the native speakers they talked to, *dont* included: 1 in 215 against 3 in 299 (Larrivée 2018).

### Where studies disagree

- **Fixed phrases or not.** Blanche-Benveniste says spoken *dont* is confined to a few formulas; Abeillé et al. say it isn't. Both are partly right. *parler* alone is half to 71% of the verb uses, but "whose" *dont* is a real quarter of spoken uses.
- **How systematic the non-standard relatives are.** Guiraud and Gadet describe a system with four parts. Exhaustive counts find the resumptive type almost absent with verbs that take a preposition: at most a few per thousand *que* relatives. Part of the difference is method, illustrative examples against full counts. Part may be the recordings: most corpora are interviews, where speakers monitor themselves (Su & Donati 2023).

## Corpus check

Transcripts only, from the spoken part of [CEFC-ORFEO](https://www.ortolang.fr/market/corpora/cefc-orfeo) recorded in France (3.2 million words; see [`../README.md`](../README.md)) and its written subcorpora. *dont* and *que* sound different, so a transcriber is unlikely to have turned one into the other, and no audio check was done.

**Rates** ([`rates.py`](rates.py), [`results/rates.csv`](results/rates.csv)). For the share column, *qui, que* and *où* count as relatives when they're tagged as relatives and come right after a noun or pronoun.

| | words | *dont* | per million | share of *qui/que/où/dont* |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| speech, France | 3.25 M | 533 | 164 | 1.6% |
| edited writing (books, press, science) | 5.45 M | 5,545 | 1,017 | 12.8% |
| online writing (tweets, Wikipedia talk, SMS, a chat) | 1.20 M | 598 | 498 | 9.5% |

Spoken subcorpora run from 38 per million (DECODA, phone calls to a transport help line) to 314 (CRFP).

**What *dont* is used for**, in 100 random *dont* from speech and 100 from edited writing, sorted by hand ([`results/dont_sample.csv`](results/dont_sample.csv)). The spoken shares match Blanche-Benveniste & Martin's and Abeillé et al.'s.

| use | speech | writing |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| verb or adjective + *de* (*dont je parle*) | 47% (~77 per million) | 38% (~390) |
| *la façon / manière dont* | 14% (~22) | 7% (~70) |
| "whose" (*dont le nom m'échappe*) | 23% (~37) | 43% (~440) |
| "including" (*dont deux enfants*) | 11% (~18) | 9% (~90) |
| unclear | 5% | 3% |

**dont against que with 14 verbs and expressions that take *de*** ([`contexts.py`](contexts.py), [`summarize.py`](summarize.py), [`results/strategies.csv`](results/strategies.csv)). The search took every *dont* with one of them, and every *que* after a noun or pronoun with one of them within seven words and no *de* after it. Every hit was checked by hand: [`results/judged.csv`](results/judged.csv) lists each verdict that isn't the default. Questions, conjunction *que* (*c'est pour ça que j'en parle*, "that's why I mention it") and *que* as the verb's object (*la langue que je parle*, "the language I speak") were dropped. That leaves 217 relative clauses:

- 177 with *dont*, 2 of them with an extra *en* (*dont on en parle*);
- 34 with *que* and no *de*, like *c'est le mec que je vous ai pas parlé* ("that's the guy I didn't tell you about");
- 1 with *que* + *en*: *des trucs que je m'en souviens* ("things I remember");
- 5 others: *c'est de ça que vous parlez* ("that's what you're talking about"), *c'est pas de ce que je voulais parler*, *les personnes de qui ils ont parlé*.

| verb | *dont* | *que* | other |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| *parler de* | 108 | 9 | 5 |
| *avoir besoin de* | 19 | 13 | 0 |
| *se souvenir de* | 13 | 3 | 0 |
| *s'occuper de*, *se servir de*, *faire partie de* | 25 | 2 | 0 |
| *avoir envie de* | 0 | 5 | 0 |

With *avoir envie*, speakers say *ce que j'ai envie de faire* instead, about 35 times. The search favours *dont*, since *que* had to follow a noun or pronoun. Holding *dont* to the same condition leaves 131, so *que* takes 16–21%. 10 of the 177 *dont* are a speaker correcting *que* or *qui* to *dont* (*du chanteur que… dont on parlera*); none go the other way. In edited writing, *dont* with the same verbs comes 51 times per million words, against 55 in speech. That figure is from 280 hits with a hand-checked sample of 40, about 95% right.

**Who** ([`results/cases.csv`](results/cases.csv), [`results/speakers.csv`](results/speakers.csv)).

- **Education and age.** The 35 *que* cases come from 27 speakers. Of the 23 whose education is recorded, 18 went to university, the same share as in the corpus as a whole. 16 of the 17 with a known age are 21–60.
- **Situation.** The cases cluster in relaxed talk among students and young adults (TUFS: 11 *que* to 23 *dont*) and in work talk: meetings, a help-line agent, a university office. Interviews have few (CFPP2000: 2 to 51).
- ***dont* itself.** Compared with what their subcorpus would predict, speakers aged 16–20 say *dont* at 0.4 times the rate and over-60s at 1.4 times. Speakers with secondary or primary education say it at 0.5–0.6 times and university-educated speakers at 1.1.

**Limits.** The *que* search misses clauses where the verb is more than seven words from *que* or the noun isn't next to it. Counts per verb are small, and age and education are missing for a third of the words.
