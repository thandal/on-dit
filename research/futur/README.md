# Futur proche and futur simple

**Question.** Section 5.6 makes the futur proche (*je vais partir*, "I'm going to leave") the everyday way to talk about the future, and 6.4 says you'll still hear the futur simple (*je partirai*, "I'll leave") all the time, especially in promises and predictions (*je t'appellerai*, "I'll call you"), in negatives (*je le ferai pas*, "I won't do it") and after *quand* ("when") about the future. How often do speakers in France use each, and are those the contexts that favour the futur simple?

**Answer.** The futur proche is the majority form, but the futur simple is far from rare: about 40% of futures in conversation recorded in France since the 1980s, between about 20% for young Parisians and 60% in Orléans in 1968. Two things favour the futur simple. Negation, especially with *jamais* ("never"), *rien* ("nothing"), *plus* ("no more"): about 8 in 10 negated futures with those words are futur simple, against about half with plain *pas* and 4 in 10 without negation. And the verb: *être*, *avoir*, *pouvoir*, *savoir* and *falloir* are futur simple two thirds to three quarters of the time (*ce sera*, "it'll be", *il y aura*, "there'll be", *on pourra*, "we'll be able to", *il faudra*, "we'll have to"), while *faire*, *dire*, *prendre* and most other verbs take the futur proche two to one or more. Promises and predictions aren't a reliable cue: studies of France disagree on whether meaning or distance in time matters at all, and in our count the futur simple is rarest with *je*. After *quand* there's a slight preference for the futur simple.

**For the grammar.** 5.6 stands. 6.4's list of contexts should change:

- Replace "in promises and predictions" with the verbs: the futur simple is the usual future of être, avoir, pouvoir and falloir (*ce sera*, *il y aura*, *on pourra*, *il faudra*). With *je* and an object pronoun, typical of promises (*je vais t'appeler*, "I'll call you"), the futur proche is four times as common in our count. The 5.1 table row and the sentence in 5.6 that say "promises, predictions" would follow.
- Keep negatives, but say it's strongest with *jamais*, *rien* and *plus* (*ça changera rien*, *je le ferai jamais*); with *pas* both are common (*je le ferai pas*, *je vais pas le faire*).
- Keep *quand*, adding that the futur proche is heard there too (*quand tu vas être grand*).
- Add *il y aura* ("there'll be") and *il faudra* ("we'll have to") to the fixed phrases; they are the second and third commonest futur simple forms, after *ce sera / ça sera*. *on en reparlera* is rare (12 cases) but harmless.
- Optional: *on va dire* ("let's say", a hedge) is very frequent (491 cases) and could go in chapter 11 as a discourse word.

## What the research says

The choice between the two futures is one of the most studied variables in French, mostly in Canada. The studies count only clauses that refer to the future and leave out fixed phrases, habits (*le lundi on va faire les courses*, "on Mondays we do the shopping") and *aller* meaning "go somewhere to".

### Canada

- **Quebec and Ontario.** The futur proche has been the majority form since the 19th century: about 56% in recordings of Quebec speakers born then, 73% in Ottawa-Hull in the 1980s, with the futur simple at about 20% and the present at 7% ([Poplack & Turpin 1999](https://doi.org/10.1515/prbs.1999.11.1.133); [Poplack & Dion 2009](https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.0.0149)). Montreal: 10% futur simple in 1971, 15.5% in 1984 ([Wagner & Sankoff 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954394511000111)); Ontario: 11% ([Grimm & Nadasdi 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269510000335)).
- **Negation decides.** In these varieties a negated future is almost always futur simple: 586 of 588 in Montreal. This holds back to the 19th century. Grammars since the 16th century have tied the choice to distance in time; in speech it matters little ([Poplack & Dion 2009](https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.0.0149)). It isn't about splitting *vais … partir* with another word either: with object pronouns or other adverbs in between, the futur proche is still used 85% of the time (Jarmasz 2007, cited there). Poplack & Dion conclude that the futur simple has become entrenched in negative contexts and is otherwise a mark of careful style.
- **Age and class.** Montrealers use more futur simple as they get older and the higher their social class ([Wagner & Sankoff 2011](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954394511000111)). Where French is a minority language, the futur simple is lost even under negation ([Grimm 2015](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/handle/10315/32175)).
- **Acadian French** is the exception: 38–53% futur simple, and distance in time matters more than negation ([King & Nadasdi 2003](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269503001157); [Comeau 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959269514000301); rates as summarized by Roberts 2012).

### France

- **Rate.** 41% futur simple in interviews from Brittany, Paris, the Lot and the Minervois recorded in the 1980s ([Roberts 2012](https://repository.upenn.edu/pwpl/vol18/iss2/12)); 39% in CFPP2000 (Paris, 2000s) and 21.5% among young speakers in Multicultural Paris French ([Liang, Burnett & Amsili 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/cnj.2025.6)); in Orléans, 58% in 1968–71 against 28% in 2008 ([Abouda & Skrovec 2017](https://doi.org/10.4000/corela.4804)); 38% in Vimeu, Picardy ([Villeneuve & Comeau 2016](https://doi.org/10.1353/cjl.2016.0025)). So the futur simple is much more alive in France than in Quebec, and the Orléans figures suggest it has been losing ground.
- **Negation favours it, less strongly than in Quebec.** [Roberts (2012)](https://repository.upenn.edu/pwpl/vol18/iss2/12): 62.5% futur simple in negatives against 38.5% in affirmatives (71% when *ne* is kept); negation was the one factor that survived once speaker and verb were controlled for. [Liang, Burnett & Amsili (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1017/cnj.2025.6) split negation by word. In CFPP2000: 36.5% futur simple in affirmatives, 47.5% with *pas*, 82% with *jamais*, *rien*, *plus*, *personne*. Among young Parisians, *pas* makes no difference at all (22% against 20%), while those words still do (62%). So the French effect comes mostly from those negative words, not from *pas*.
- **Distance in time: studies disagree.** No effect in Roberts (2012) or Liang et al. (2025), nor of time adverbs, *quand*, or an *if*-condition (Roberts). But in informal conversations between native speakers, [Gudmestad, Edmonds, Donaldson & Carmichael (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1017/cnj.2017.41) find that distance and the presence of a time expression do predict the choice; in Orléans, things already under way or close are mostly futur proche and distant ones mostly futur simple ([Abouda & Skrovec 2017](https://doi.org/10.4000/corela.4804)); and in Vimeu, distance is the only linguistic factor, with events within the hour strongly favouring the futur proche ([Villeneuve & Comeau 2016](https://doi.org/10.1353/cjl.2016.0025)).
- **Meaning.** Using corpora from southern France and a questionnaire, [Lacross (2018)](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/18926) argues that the futur proche presents the event as certain from present evidence, and the futur simple carries a promise or the speaker's willingness. That supports "promises", but it hasn't been tested by counting.
- **Other factors.** Formal *vous* (63% futur simple against 40%) and a university degree (56% against 38%) favour the futur simple in Roberts (2012); third-person subjects and vague time adverbs in CFPP2000 (Fleury & Branca-Rosoff 2010, cited by Liang et al.). Verbs differ a lot: *avoir* is futur simple 80% of the time in Roberts's data.
- ***on va dire*** ("let's say") has grown since the 1970s from a future of *dire* into a hedge, which accounts for part of the futur proche's rise in Orléans ([Abouda & Skrovec 2016](https://ddd.uab.cat/record/171402); [Abouda, Skrovec & Badin 2025](https://doi.org/10.3917/lang.238.0137)).

## Corpus check (transcript-only)

None of the studies of France gives figures for each verb or for the fixed phrases, and the largest has about 3,300 futures. [`extract.py`](extract.py) counts both futures in the transcripts of [CEFC-ORFEO](https://www.ortolang.fr/market/corpora/cefc-orfeo) recorded in France (3.2 million words; see [`../README.md`](../README.md)):

- **futur simple:** a finite verb whose form is its own future stem plus -ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont, checked against the tagger's lemma (*sera* is *être* + a; *préférez* and *rentrez* are presents, not futures).
- **futur proche:** *vais*, *vas*, *va*, *allons*, *allez*, *vont* after a subject, then an infinitive, with only pronouns, *ne*, a negative word or a short adverb between. Imperatives (*va voir !*) have no subject and are left out.

Unlike the studies above, it doesn't remove habits, motion or hypotheticals. In a hand check of 100 futurs proches, 5 to 8 weren't futures (*quand je vais promener mes petits-enfants*, "when I go and walk my grandchildren"). Left out: *on va dire* (491), the futur antérieur (*il aura fini*, 146), and DECODA's call-centre phrase *(name) va vous répondre* (749).

**Result: 7,382 futur simple, 10,277 futur proche (42% futur simple)**, in line with Roberts (41%) and CFPP2000 (39%).

| | futur simple | futur proche | % futur simple |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| affirmative | 6,295 | 9,542 | 40 |
| negated with *pas* | 765 | 685 | 53 |
| negated with *jamais*, *rien*, *plus*, *personne* | 246 | 48 | 84 |
| after *quand*, *lorsque*, *dès que* | 202 | 179 | 53 |
| subject *je* | 1,418 | 3,238 | 30 |

The negation effect holds within each common verb: *être* 62% → 77%, *avoir* 74% → 93%, *pouvoir* 72% → 87%, *faire* 29% → 43%. By verb ([`results/by_verb.csv`](results/by_verb.csv)): *avoir* 77%, *savoir* 77%, *pouvoir* 76%, *falloir* 70%, *être* 64%, *voir* 47%, *aller* 44%, *dire* 32%, *faire* 30%, *donner* 24%, *mettre* 23%, *prendre* 21%, *regarder* 11%, *chercher* 6%. Eight verbs (être, avoir, pouvoir, faire, falloir, voir, aller, dire) make up 64% of all futur simple forms. Fixed phrases ([`results/fixed.csv`](results/fixed.csv)):

| futur simple / futur proche | futur simple | futur proche | % futur simple |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| *ce sera, ça sera / ça va être* | 641 | 409 | 61 |
| *il faudra / il va falloir* | 419 | 182 | 70 |
| *il y aura / il va y avoir* | 324 | 68 | 83 |
| *on verra / on va voir* | 208 | 115 | 64 |
| *tu verras, vous verrez / tu vas voir, vous allez voir* | 123 | 104 | 54 |
| *ça ira / ça va aller* | 42 | 32 | 57 |

By situation ([`results/by_context.csv`](results/by_context.csv)): storytelling 61%, work meetings 52%, conversation 43%, interviews 36%, phone calls to the transport help line 33%. With *je* + *te*, *vous* or *lui* ("I'll tell you", "I'll send you"), the futur proche wins four to one (*je vais vous donner le numéro*), which is why "promises" isn't a good cue here, though many of these are immediate.

**Limits.** These are transcripts. *je ferai* and *je ferais* sound the same for many speakers, so the transcriber chooses: in a sample of 60 *je* futures, about one in six looked like a conditionnel (*j'aimerai savoir*, "I'd like to know"). Leaving *je* out changes little (affirmative 44%, negated 62%). Nothing here codes distance in time, so it can't settle that disagreement. The two futures differ by whole words, so there is no point checking the audio.
