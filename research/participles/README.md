# Participle agreement with avoir

**Question.** The "More: agreement with avoir" note in 5.4 says that with avoir, the participle agrees with a direct object that comes before the verb (*la lettre que j'ai écrite*, "the letter I wrote"; *ta veste, je l'ai prise*, "your jacket, I took it"), that you only hear it on participles ending in a consonant, and that "many speakers leave it out in casual speech". How often do speakers in France actually say the feminine? Should the grammar teach it as the norm, as optional, or as mostly absent?

**Answer.** Optional. In conversation recorded in France, speakers make the audible agreement with avoir somewhere between a third and a half of the time; the masculine (*la lettre que j'ai écrit*) is at least as common. The studies of France all find this variation, and the largest finds no sign that it's changing: it's a stable choice that depends on the speaker and the situation.

- **More agreement:** in careful or public speech, from speakers with more education, when the participle ends the sentence (*c'est une habitude que j'ai prise*), and with *faire* (*faite*).
- **Less agreement:** when something follows the participle (*la plainte que j'avais mis contre le chauffeur*, "the complaint I'd made against the driver"), and with *me, te, nous, vous* as the object (a woman saying *il m'a pris pour une autre*, "he took me for someone else").
- **With être it's close to automatic:** over 90% (*elle est morte*, "she's dead"; *la porte était ouverte*). With reflexives, where *se* is the object, it's about three in four (*elle s'est mise à pleurer*, "she started crying").
- **Listeners hardly react:** in the one perception test, hearing the agreement or not barely changed how people judged a speaker.

**For the grammar.** Keep the note in 5.4 as a "More" box, but make it say the agreement is optional rather than the rule some speakers break. A possible wording:

> With avoir, the participle can also agree with a direct object that comes before the verb: *la lettre que j'ai écrite*, *ta veste, je l'ai prise*. You only hear it on participles ending in a consonant (*prise, mise, faite, écrite, ouverte*), and in conversation it's optional: *la lettre que j'ai écrit* is at least as common, and both sound normal. You hear the agreement more in careful speech, and most when the participle ends the sentence. Written French always makes it (A5).

The rest of "When the participle changes" stands: agreement with être is as regular as 5.4 says. Reflexives are a little less regular (about three in four), not enough to need a note.

## What the research says

Most participles sound the same in the masculine and the feminine (*mangé / mangée*), so the question only arises for the few that end in a silent consonant: *fait, pris, mis, dit, écrit, ouvert, offert, peint* and their compounds (Tanase 1976 lists them). Robillard counts about 99 such verbs, of which 30 occur in her corpus. Even these are rare with a feminine object before them: Blanche-Benveniste found 48 usable cases in 2 million words and judged that a corpus under 10 million words is too small to study them. Every study below is small.

### How often

| study | data | avoir: feminine said | être |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Audibert-Gibier 1992 | about 170 examples, mostly heard and noted down by the author over a year (1988), a few from the GARS corpus (Aix) | after *que* 24%; after *l', la, les* 33%; after *me, te* 1 of 6 | 61% (23 cases) |
| Blanche-Benveniste 2006 | 2 million words: GARS (Aix), the *Français de référence* corpus, and recordings of political programmes | 36 of 48 | |
| Gaucher 2013 | 5.8 million words: Valibel (Belgium), PFC (France, Belgium, Switzerland), CFPP2000 (Paris), C-ORAL-ROM (France) | 158 of 317 (50%): 37–40% in PFC, CFPP2000 and C-ORAL-ROM, 57% in Valibel | 92%; reflexives 77%; participle alone 93% |
| Robillard 2016 | Ottawa–Hull (Canada), 3,566 tokens of participles with an audible feminine | "virtually nonexistent" once *fait* is set aside | |

The figures for France cluster around a third to two fifths in ordinary interviews and conversation. Blanche-Benveniste's high figure includes politicians and other public speakers, who agree far more (below). Robillard's near-zero is for Canadian French and may not carry over. The two older positions, that agreement is still alive in everyday speech (Audibert-Gibier 1992) and that it is disappearing from productive use (Smith 1995, 1996), both find support depending on which corpus you look at. Gaucher's age figures show no steady decline, and he treats agreement as a stable variable tied to education and job rather than one being lost.

### What makes agreement more likely

**Register and education.** This is the strongest effect. Gaucher's speakers agree 20% of the time if they stopped school before the *lycée*, 40% if they went up to the *bac*, 59% with higher education. By job, people who address audiences for a living ("public speech specialists", such as politicians) agree 75% of the time, people with no such demands 31%. Blanche-Benveniste likewise finds more agreement in public speech, and Blanche-Benveniste et al. (1990) suggest that two years of study after the *bac* works as a threshold. Gaucher can't separate the speaker from the formality of the recording: public speakers were often recorded in formal settings.

**Position.** Agreement is commonest when nothing follows the participle in its clause. Audibert-Gibier: clitics 33% overall but 64% when the participle ends the clause; *que* 24% overall, 34% at the end. Gaucher: 63% at the end against 43% when something follows. Audibert-Gibier sees this as an extension of the position rule itself; Gaucher leaves open whether it is that, or the participle simply being more noticeable at the end.

**The object.** *me, te, nous, vous* as the object make agreement rare: Audibert-Gibier 1 of 6, Gaucher 8 of 37 (22%), against 51% for *l'* and *les*. The studies disagree about *que* against *l'/les*: Audibert-Gibier finds *que* lower, Gaucher finds it higher (56% against 51%). Gaucher also finds more agreement the further the antecedent is from the participle (45% within two syllables, 58–71% beyond five).

**The participle.** *faire* is special. Audibert-Gibier finds *faite* in 64% of cases (80% at the end of the clause); Gaucher has *faite* 53% against 42% for *prise* and *mise*. Robillard argues that in Ottawa French *faite* [fɛt] has become a form of its own, said even where nothing should agree (*l'hygiéniste a pas faite beaucoup de cours*), and that once *faire* is removed, almost no real agreement is left. Blanche-Benveniste suggests speakers avoid feminines that sound like a noun or adjective (*tout le monde l'a plainte*, "everyone pitied her", sounds odd because *plainte* means "complaint"). Gaucher's counts are mixed: such participles agree more often than others overall, but *prise* (also "plug", "catch") agrees least, 34%.

**Meaning.** Blanche-Benveniste suggests agreement is likelier when the passé composé describes a resulting state (*la faute qu'on avait faite, on la recopiait*, "the mistake we'd made, we copied it out") than a single event (*ils l'ont ouverte le 15 août à midi*, "they opened it on 15 August at noon"). Gaucher 2015 finds the difference (55% against 43%) in the expected direction but too small to be sure of.

**Speaker sex and age.** Gaucher finds men agreeing more than women (56% against 43%), the reverse of the usual pattern for standard forms; part of the gap comes from women's *me* cases, which rarely agree. By age, agreement peaks among 25–39-year-olds (64%) and is lowest among the over-60s (37%). Neither is clear-cut.

**Perception.** Gaucher's matched-guise test played listeners the same speakers with and without agreement. Their judgements of the speakers (education, intelligence, social class) barely changed.

**Children.** In Quebec, children acquire the agreement late, probably only at school age, and default to the masculine before then (Pirvulescu & Belzil 2008; Pirvulescu 2010). Canadian, but it fits the picture of a feature carried by schooling rather than by everyday input.

**Reliability of transcripts.** Branca (2005) warns that transcribers may add or drop the agreement. Gaucher checked 24 Belgian occurrences against the audio: 21 matched, one agreement was heard but not transcribed, one transcribed but not heard. Liaison is a second trap: a masculine can link into a following vowel (*vous avez été mis‿en examen*, heard on France Inter), which sounds like the feminine, so the studies set aside such cases.

I couldn't check what Gadet's descriptions of ordinary French say about this; Gaucher draws on Gadet (2007) only for the general point that, of two equivalent forms, the one with more sound in it tends to be the more formal (*faite* against *fait*, like *quatre* against *quat'*).

## Corpus check (transcript-only)

The transcripts of [CEFC-ORFEO](https://www.ortolang.fr/market/corpora/cefc-orfeo) recorded in France (3.2 million words; see [`../README.md`](../README.md)) give a cheap comparison. They overlap Gaucher's data in part (CFPP2000, C-ORAL-ROM). [`extract.py`](extract.py) finds avoir or être + a participle whose feminine adds a consonant ([`participles.py`](participles.py)) and sorts each hit by what it should agree with; noun gender comes from the corpus itself ([`gender.py`](gender.py)), since the tags don't record it. Every avoir hit was then read in context by hand ([`results/hand_coded.csv`](results/hand_coded.csv)): 42 of 119 hits after *que* were dropped because *que* isn't the participle's object (*la première fois que j'ai fait ça*, "the first time I did that"), and *l'/les* hits were kept only where a feminine noun nearby is clearly the referent, by the same search for both spellings. [`transcripts.py`](transcripts.py) counts them.

| context | standard | written feminine | written masculine | feminine |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| *elle(s)* + être | feminine | 126 | 6 | 95% |
| feminine noun + être | feminine | 86 | 7 | 92% |
| *elle* + *se* + être, *se* the object | feminine | 23 | 7 | 77% |
| feminine noun + *que* + avoir | feminine | 29 | 53 | 35% |
| *l'* / *les* (feminine) + avoir | feminine | 35 | 25 | 58% |

In contexts where only the masculine is possible (avoir + participle + object, *ce que j'ai fait*, *il est mort*), transcribers never wrote a feminine (0 of 2,802). Per participle: [`results/transcript_counts.csv`](results/transcript_counts.csv).

Together the avoir rows give 64 feminine out of 142 (45%), from 126 speakers, close to Gaucher's figures, and être and reflexives match his 92% and 77%. Both forms turn up in the same kinds of sentences: *c'est une erreur qu'ils ont fait* ("it's a mistake they made"), *la télé est encore allumée? — non, je l'ai éteint* ("is the TV still on? — no, I turned it off"), against *c'est une photo qu'on a prise à Marseille*, *la fiche de lecture, tu l'as faite? — non, je l'ai pas encore faite* ("the reading report, have you done it? — no, not yet"). Speakers with higher education get the feminine a little more often (36 of 70) than those who stopped at *collège* or *lycée* (8 of 24). And, as Malherbe already complained in the 17th century ("on ne dit pas *je l'ai faite venir*"), *faite* turns up before an infinitive where the standard keeps *fait*: *je l'ai faite saigner du nez* ("I made her nose bleed").

**Limits.** These are transcripts. Transcribers write standard spelling and could add an *-e* they didn't hear or leave out one they did; the 78 masculines with a feminine object show they don't correct systematically, but a spoken difference of one final consonant is easy to normalize either way. The relative-clause search misses some patterns (inverted subjects, long subjects), and the *l'/les* count misses referents mentioned far back. Liaison isn't set aside here: a masculine linked into a vowel (*pris‿en photo*) may have been heard, and written, as a feminine. An audio check along the lines of [`../ils-stems/`](../ils-stems/) was started and dropped.
