# The subjunctive after its triggers

**Question.** Section 6.5 teaches the subjunctive after a short list of phrases (*il faut que*, *pour que*, *je veux que*…) and gives six verbs with a subjunctive of their own: être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir, savoir. Spoken French is sometimes said to be losing the subjunctive. Do speakers in France actually use it after those phrases? And which subjunctive forms does a learner really need?

**Answer.** Yes, they use it. Studies of speech in France agree that after the few triggers that carry most of the subjunctive (*il faut que*, *vouloir que*, *pour que*, *avant que*, *sans que*), the subjunctive is used 90–100% of the time. The rate was the same in Orléans in 1968 as in Paris and Orléans in the 2000s. Variation is real, but it sits elsewhere: with rarer and "optional" triggers (*je pense pas que*, "I don't think that", *à moins que*, "unless", *après que*, "after"), and in Canadian French where French is a minority language. The forms that matter are few: être, avoir, aller and faire carry about three quarters of all subjunctives in the research, mostly in set strings like *que ce soit* ("that it be"), *qu'il y ait* ("that there be"), *que je fasse* ("that I do"), *que j'aille* ("that I go"). vouloir's own subjunctive (*veuille*) hardly occurs.

**For the grammar.** 6.5 stands, and so does its choice of six verbs and its point that most subjunctives sound like the ils form. Two small changes are worth making:

- Add *qu'il y ait* ("that there's") to 6.5, as an example or in practice. After *que ce soit*, it's the commonest subjunctive in our count.
- In [`listes.html`](../../grammaire/listes.html#subjonctif), *à moins que* and *il est possible que* could carry a note that the indicative is also heard after them. Nothing else in the list needs changing.

## What the research says

### The subjunctive lives on a few triggers

The main body of work is Shana Poplack's on Canadian French. Grammars prescribe the subjunctive after hundreds of verbs and conjunctions; in speech it turns up after a few dozen, and a handful carry nearly all of it ([Poplack 1992](https://doi.org/10.1075/cilt.74.17pop); [Poplack, Lealess & Dion 2013](https://doi.org/10.1515/probus-2013-0005)). In Quebec French:

- *falloir que* is about two thirds of all verbal triggers, and takes the subjunctive 89% of the time in 20th-century speech, 94% in the 21st.
- With all the other verbal triggers together, the rate stays at 51–55% from the 19th century to today. The apparent rise of the subjunctive over time is entirely *falloir*.
- A few triggers (*falloir*, *vouloir*, *aimer*, *pour que*) and a few verbs (être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir) make up most of the data. When both come together, the subjunctive is near certain: 92% in the 20th century, 99% in the 21st.
- The meanings grammars attribute to the subjunctive (doubt, wish, emotion, unreality) don't predict where it's used. Which trigger it is does. A comparison of spoken French, Italian, Portuguese and Spanish finds that the trigger's identity is the main predictor in all four, and that only in Spanish do the semantic classes of triggers matter; French has gone furthest in making the subjunctive a mere marker of certain constructions ([Poplack et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110365955-009)).

### France: the same system, stable over 40 years

[Kastronic (2016)](https://ruor.uottawa.ca/handle/10393/35493) applied the same method to France: ESLO1 (Orléans, 1968–70), and CFPP2000 (Paris) and ESLO2 (Orléans) from the 2000s. Like most such studies, she counts only verbs whose subjunctive differs from the indicative.

| trigger | Orléans 1968–70 | Paris and Orléans, 2000s |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| *falloir que* | 98% (216/221) | 95% (120/126) |
| *vouloir que* | 96% | 90% |
| *aimer que* | 95% | 79% |
| *penser que*, negated (*je pense pas que*) | 72% | 58% |
| *croire que*, negated | 72% | 40% (2/5) |
| all verbal triggers | 71% | 70% |

Among conjunctions (1968–70): *pour que* 100%, *avant que* 100%, *sans que* 100%, *bien que* 86%, *à moins que* 85%, *après que* 67% (*bien que* and *après que* on fewer than 10 cases each). The overall rate of about 70% comes from triggers the grammar doesn't teach as subjunctive triggers: negated opinion verbs, *voir que*, *dire que*, *comprendre que*, *il me semble que*, where 5–50% is normal.

Points where France differs from Quebec:

- *falloir que* is 30% of verbal triggers in France against 65% in Quebec. Necessity is more often said with *il faut* + infinitive, *devoir* or *être obligé de* in France, so Quebec figures overstate how much of the subjunctive hangs on *il faut que*.
- Outside the top few conjunctions, the rate is lower in France (51% against 70%); outside *falloir*, verbal triggers are at about 60% in France and 51–53% in Quebec. The core is just as solid in both.
- Nothing changed in France between 1968 and the 2000s. In Quebec, *falloir que* has become more firmly subjunctive.

Earlier studies of France say much the same, with smaller samples. In Sand's sample of ESLO1, *falloir* took the subjunctive 98% of the time (61/62), *vouloir* and other wanting verbs 100%, emotion verbs 90%, opinion verbs 50%, *pour que* 100%; être, avoir, faire and aller were 63–77% of the subjunctive forms in the categories he checked. Young speakers in Caen used it less (60% against 85% for ESLO adults), which Sand reads as age, not change (Sand 1983, 2003, summarized in Kastronic 2016, ch. 3). In the GARS corpus of spoken French (Lim 1989, in Blanche-Benveniste et al. 1990), 35% of subjunctives came after impersonal phrases like *il faut que*, 28% after personal verbs like *je veux que*, 21% after *pour que*, *avant que*, *à moins que*. Studies reporting very low rates (5%) either counted every subordinate clause, most of which never take the subjunctive, or drew examples from dictionaries (Bieler 1972, Chauveau 1998, both discussed by Kastronic).

### Where it is being lost

Decline is documented in Ontario, among speakers who use French less than English. The more restricted a speaker's French, the less subjunctive, and much of the drop comes from *il faut que* giving way to *devoir* ([Laurier 1989](https://icrml.ca/en/research-and-publications/references/item/76975-le-subjonctif-dans-le-parler-franco-ontarien-un-mode-en-voie-de-disparition); [Grimm 2015](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/handle/10315/32175)). That is a language-contact effect and doesn't carry over to France.

### après que

The rule is the indicative, but the subjunctive is spreading. In speech the only counts I found are tiny: [Canut & Ledegen (1998)](https://doi.org/10.3406/lsoc.1998.2823) gathered 11 cases, 8 of them subjunctive, partly from examples noted by ear, so not a rate; Kastronic's 67% rests on fewer than 10. In writing, it depends on the genre: 71% subjunctive in readers' comments online, 25–30% in press articles and books ([Gustafsson 2024](https://ebooks.au.dk/aul/catalog/book/522)); 41% on the French web against 5% in literature ([Ruotsalainen 2019](https://www.doria.fi/handle/10024/169327)). The grammar's note ("you'll also hear the subjunctive after *après que*") matches this.

### Which verbs carry a distinct subjunctive

For one-form verbs, including every -er verb, the subjunctive sounds like the present (*il faut que je parle*), so studies leave them out. What's left is dominated by four verbs: être, avoir, aller and faire are 73% of the verbs under subjunctive triggers in Orléans 1968–70 (Kastronic 2016, table 3.32) and the top four in Quebec too. Kastronic's other verbs with distinct forms (*dire*, *prendre*, *venir*, *comprendre*, *écrire*, *sortir*, *mettre*) take the subjunctive at least as often (82% overall). Those are exactly the verbs 6.5 handles with the ils-form rule.

## Corpus check (transcript-only)

The literature doesn't give a per-trigger, per-verb count for the grammar's own list in present-day France, or say which forms a learner hears most. [`extract.py`](extract.py) fills that gap from the transcripts of [CEFC-ORFEO](https://www.ortolang.fr/market/corpora/cefc-orfeo) recorded in France (3.2 million words; see [`../README.md`](../README.md)). It finds each trigger + *que* + verb and counts only verbs whose subjunctive sounds different from the indicative for that person ([`forms.py`](forms.py)): the six and vouloir, two-stem verbs with je, tu, il or on (*qu'il parte* / *il part*), and any verb with vous (*que vous partiez* / *vous partez*). It sets aside *j'ai* / *j'aie*, which many speakers say the same way, and ils with two-stem verbs. Every one of the 131 indicatives found was read by hand ([`results/indicative_checked.csv`](results/indicative_checked.csv)): 61 were real, 61 weren't a trigger + verb (restarts, *c'est après qu'on a vu*, *qu'est-ce que*…), and 9 were *est* or *es* written for the homophone *ait* or *aies* (*il faut qu'on est un plan* = *qu'on ait*), which count as subjunctive.

**Result: 1,967 subjunctive, 61 indicative (97%)**, from 267 speakers; the 61 come from 29 of them. Of about 280 subjunctive hits read by hand, one wasn't a trigger + verb (*que ce soit* meaning "whether it's").

| trigger | subjunctive | indicative | % subjunctive |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| *il faut / faudrait / fallait / faudra que* | 1,040 | 15 | 99 |
| *pour que* | 325 | 6 | 98 |
| *vouloir que* | 143 | 4 | 97 |
| *avant que* | 66 | 4 | 94 |
| *sans que* | 36 | 0 | 100 |
| *il est possible que* | 16 | 3 | 84 |
| *c'est dommage que* | 16 | 5 | 76 |
| *à moins que* | 21 | 9 | 70 |
| *après que* | 13 | 1 | 93 |

All triggers: [`results/by_trigger.csv`](results/by_trigger.csv); per verb: [`results/by_verb.csv`](results/by_verb.csv); trigger + verb: [`results/combos.csv`](results/combos.csv). The real indicatives include *il faut que tu fais un plan* ("you need to make a plan"), *pour que les gens peuvent se promener* ("so people can go for walks"), *il faut que vous récupérez* ("you need to collect"), *à moins que tu l'as, le numéro* ("unless you've got it, the number") and *j'ai peur que c'est pas ça* ("I'm afraid it's not that").

- *il faut que* and its tenses carry 53% of all subjunctives, *pour que* 17%, *vouloir que* 7%.
- The six verbs of 6.5 carry 69% of them; être, avoir, aller and faire 59%. vouloir's *veuille* occurs twice.
- The commonest forms: *que ce soit*, *que ça soit* and *qu'il soit* (304 together), *qu'il y ait* (156), *qu'il fasse* (110), *que je fasse* (98), *que j'aille* (86), *qu'il puisse* (78), *qu'il ait* (74).
- In 43% of trigger + verb cases the verb's subjunctive sounds like its present (*il faut que je trouve*, *pour qu'on voie*): the "without noticing" case 6.5 describes.

These figures sit close to Kastronic's for the same triggers. The overall rate is higher than her 70% only because the grammar's list leaves out the variable triggers.

**Limits.** Transcribers write standard spelling and might have written a subjunctive they didn't hear. They did write 61 indicatives, so they don't correct systematically, and most of the pairs counted here differ by a whole vowel or syllable (*soit* / *est*, *aille* / *va*, *fasse* / *fait*), which is hard to mishear. An audio check like [`../ils-stems/score_audio.py`](../ils-stems/score_audio.py) would only matter for two-stem verbs, where the difference is mostly a final consonant (*parte* / *part*); they are about a quarter of the count (536) and show 2% indicative. Even if one in ten of their subjunctives were really an indicative the transcriber corrected, the overall rate would still be about 94%, so it isn't worth doing. The trigger finder misses some cases (a relative clause between *que* and the verb, for example), which shouldn't bias the rate.

```sh
sh ../ils-stems/fetch.sh data                     # transcripts, ~140 MB zipped
python3 extract.py data/cefc data/out             # data/out/tokens.csv
python3 summarize.py data/out/tokens.csv results  # tables; uses results/indicative_checked.csv
```

`summarize.py` warns if an indicative hit is missing from the hand check (after a change to `extract.py`, say); add it there with `ind`, `ait` or `not`.
