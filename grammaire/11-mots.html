Chapter 11Discourse words

Small words like bon, du coup and quoi don't add facts. They organise the conversation: they start, link, soften and wrap up what you say. Recognise them all. To start using them, pick bon, alors, {en fète|en fait}, du coup, quand même and quoi.

11.1 Starting and buying time

SayMeaningExample
bonright, OK: to start or close a topicbon, on‿y va ?

{bin|ben} oui and bah oui mean "well, yes, obviously". {bin|ben} non is "well, no".

quand même has two uses. After a fact, it means "still" or "anyway": il pleuvait, mais on‿{è|est} {sorti|sortis} quand même (it was raining, but we went out anyway). After a judgement, it adds weight, like "really" or "you have to admit": c'{è|est} cher quand même (it's pretty expensive, though).

{en fète|en fait}, {chui|je suis} pas libre samedi, du coup on {fè|fait} ça dimanche ?

En fait, je suis pas libre samedi, du coup on fait ça dimanche ?

Actually, I'm not free on Saturday, so shall we do it on Sunday? · en fait corrects a plan · du coup gives the result

11.3 Wrapping up and checking

SayMeaningExample
quoiat the end: you know, basicallyc'{étè|était} bizarre, quoi

quoi at the end of a sentence isn't a question. It rounds off what you've said. genre is used mostly by younger speakers.

Practice: which word?

Choose a word from 11.1 to 11.3.

  1. You want to correct what you just said: "Tuesday, I mean Wednesday." mardi, enfin mercredi
  2. There were strikes, so you came by bike. {y avè|il y avait} grève, du coup {chui|je suis} venu en vélo
  3. You're cutting a long story short. bref
  4. You want to say "actually, he isn't coming". {en fète|en fait}, il {vien|vient} pas
  5. It was raining, but you went out anyway. il pleuvait, mais on‿{è|est} {sorti|sortis} quand même

More: swearing

Two swearwords are so common in relaxed speech that you'll hear them from almost everyone:

  • merde (literally "shit"): when something goes wrong. mince is the polite version.
  • putain (literally "whore"): surprise, annoyance or emphasis, like "damn" or "bloody": putain, c'{è|est} cher !

Learn to recognize them. Said to people you don't know well, they sound crude.

Practice: reacting

React with one or two words.

  1. Your friend got the job. tant mieux, or carrément !
  2. The concert's cancelled; it doesn't matter much. tant pis
  3. Someone says the earth is flat. n'importe quoi
  4. "On se {voi|voit} à huit‿{heure|heures} ?" ça marche, or d'accord
  5. "Il a déménagé." You didn't know. ah bon ?
  6. "{T'a|Tu as} aimé le film ?" You didn't, much. bof
  7. The neighbours have been noisy all week. j'en‿ai marre

11.5 Softening and strengthening

  • Softening: un peu (a bit), plutôt (rather), je {croi|crois} and je pense (I think), peut-être (maybe), à peu près (roughly), on va dire (let's say). c'{è|est} plutôt bien (it's pretty good).
  • Strengthening: très (very), vraiment (really), vachement (really, informal), trop (really, informal; also "too"). c'{è|est} vachement bien (it's really good).

on va dire looks like the futur proche of dire (5.6), but it doesn't point to the future. It hedges a word you're not sure fits: c'{è|est}, on va dire, assez compliqué (it's, let's say, fairly complicated).

trop means "too" in standard use: c'{è|est} trop cher (it's too expensive). Younger speakers also use it for "really": c'{è|est} trop bien (it's really great). Context tells you which.
