Chapter 3Pronouns

"You" is tu or vous, depending on who you're talking to. "We" is on. Short pronouns go before the verb, and ça stands in for almost any thing or situation.

3.1 Subject pronouns

SayMeaning
je (j' before a vowel)I
tuyou: one person you know well (3.2)
vousyou: one person you don't know well, or more than one person (3.2)
il · ellehe · she; also "it", for a masculine or feminine noun

ils · ellesthey
  • il and elle mean "it" too. Use the noun's gender: {t'as*|tu as} vu ma voiture ? — elle est devant (it's out front).
  • ils is for men, masculine nouns and mixed groups. elles is only for groups that are all women or all feminine nouns.
  • il and ils sound the same before a consonant, and so do elle and elles. Before a vowel, the plural links: il a / ils‿ont, elle aime / elles‿{aime*|aiment}.

There is a second word for "we", nous. As a subject you'll hear it in formal speech, on the news and in speeches. In conversation, "we" is on.

3.2 tu or vous

French has two words for "you" talking to one person. The choice says how close you are.

Say tu toSay vous to
family and friendsadults you don't know: in shops, on the street, on the phone
children and animalspeople serving you, and people you serve
people your age in casual settings, especially young peopleolder people you've just met, including your friends' parents
colleagues, in workplaces where everyone says tuyour doctor, your landlord, officials, and usually your boss
anyone who has asked you tomore than one person, always, even friends

The default: say vous to any adult until they say tu to you or suggest switching. Starting with vous is never rude. Saying tu too early can be.

The switch is usually spoken aloud: on peut se tutoyer ? (shall we say tu?) or tu peux me tutoyer. Once you've switched, don't go back. tutoyer means to say tu to someone; vouvoyer means to say vous.

vous goes with {messieu*|monsieur} and madame: bonjour madame, vous‿avez l'heure ?

Which would you say?

  1. Your sister. tu
  2. The person at the bakery counter. vous
  3. Two friends, together. vous: always for more than one person.
  4. A six-year-old. tu

3.3 on

on takes the same verb form as il and elle (4.1). It has three meanings:

  • we, its everyday meaning: on‿y va ? on mange où ? on s'appelle demain.
  • people in general: en France, on mange tard; on dit que… (people say that…).
  • someone: on m'a volé mon vélo (someone stole my bike).

on is only a subject. For "us" and "our", use the forms that go with nous: "us" is nous (il nous‿a vus, he saw us), "our" is notre, nos (2.7), and the stressed form is also nous (3.4). Adjectives and participles are plural, though usually you can't hear it: on‿est contents, on‿est partis.

nous, on‿y va demain

We're going tomorrow. · nous for emphasis, on as the subject

3.5 Object pronouns

Object pronouns go just before the verb:

  • le or lui? Only for il, elle, ils and elles do you need to choose. Use lui and leur when the verb takes à: parler à, dire à, donner à, demander à, téléphoner à, écrire à (more).
  • le / les is the same contrast as with articles: je le vois / je les vois, "uh" versus é.
  • With two verbs, the pronoun goes before the one it belongs to, usually the infinitive: tu peux m'aider ?, je vais le faire.
  • In the passé composé, it goes before avoir or être: je l'ai vu {ièr*|hier}, on lui a dit.
  • In commands, it goes after the verb, and me and te become moi and toi: aide-moi, regarde-le, dis-lui (4.4).

Two object pronouns

me, te, nous and vous combine freely with le, la and les, and come first: tu me le prêtes ? (will you lend it to me?) With lui and leur, le, la and les are usually left out: je lui ai donné (I gave it to him).

With on as the subject, "us" is nous, which combines like me and te: il nous le prête (he's lending it to us). But when the "us" is the subject itself (ourselves, each other), the pronoun is se, as with any reflexive verb (4.5): on se le garde (we're keeping it for ourselves).

3.6 y and en

y means "there", for a place already mentioned: tu vas à Paris ? — oui, j'y vais demain. It's also in some of the most common phrases in the language:

SayMeaning
on‿y valet's go
{vas‿y*|vas-y} · {allez‿y*|allez-y}go on, go ahead
ça y estthat's it, done, finally
j'y crois pasI don't believe it

en means "some", "of it" or "of them". Use it with an amount, or to stand for du, de la or des + a noun:

  • {t'as*|tu as} des frères ? — oui, j'en‿ai deux.
  • tu veux du café ? — non merci, j'en‿ai déjà.
  • {y en‿a*|il y en a}: there is some, there are some. {y en‿a*|il y en a} plus: there's none left.

en is also part of some fixed verbs: je m'en vais (I'm leaving), j'en sais rien (no idea), on‿en parle demain (let's talk about it tomorrow). Verbs that take de work with en: see the list.

Like other object pronouns, y and en go before the verb, and they link: on‿y va, j'en‿ai.

{t'as*|tu as} des‿enfants ? — oui, j'en‿ai trois

Tu as des enfants ? — Oui, j'en ai trois.

Do you have children? — Yes, I have three. · en with an amount

  5. Tu penses au voyage ? — Oui, j'___ pense tout le temps. j'y pense

3.7 ça and c'est

ça means "that" or "it" for things, ideas and situations, whatever their gender:

  • As a subject: ça va ? ça marche. ça dépend. ça coûte combien ?
  • As an object: j'aime ça, tu veux ça ?, donne-moi ça.
  • Before être, ça becomes c': c'est, c'était.

For something in general, use ça; for a particular person or thing, use le or la: tu aimes le fromage ? — oui, j'adore ça, but tu connais Marie ? — oui, je la connais.

c'est or il est?

  • c'est + a name, or a noun with an article: c'est Marie, c'est mon frère, c'est un bon film.
  • c'est + an adjective, to judge a thing or situation: c'est bien, c'est nul, c'est pas grave. The adjective is always masculine: la soupe ? c'est bon.
  • il est or elle est + an adjective, to describe a particular person: il est sympa, elle est fatiguée.
  • il est or elle est + a job, with no article: elle est médecin. With an article, it's c'est: c'est un prof.
More: jobs with and without an article

After être (to be), a job, nationality or role normally has no article. It works like an adjective describing the person: je suis prof (I'm a teacher), elle est médecin, il est français. The same goes after devenir (to become): il veut devenir médecin.

The job takes an article when:

  • the sentence uses c'est: c'est un prof (he's a teacher).
  • the job has an adjective: c'est un bon prof (he's a good teacher), not il est un bon prof.
  • you mean one particular person: c'est le prof de Paul (he's Paul's teacher).

Everywhere else, a job is an ordinary noun with an article: un prof m'a dit… (a teacher told me…), je cherche un médecin (I'm looking for a doctor).

c'est mon frère, il est prof

That's my brother, he's a teacher. · c'est + noun · il est + job
