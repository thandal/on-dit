Chapter 5Verbs: the everyday past and future

The passé composé says what happened, the imparfait what was going on, and the futur proche what's going to happen. With the present, these are the everyday four: they do most of the work in conversation. The passé composé and the futur proche use verbs you know from chapter 4, and the imparfait adds one ending to a form you know. The only new form is the participle (5.3). The other common forms are in chapter 6.

Apart from the present, they're built in three ways:

  • A helper verb plus another form. The helper (avoir, être, aller, venir) changes with the person, and you already know it from 4.3. The other part never changes: j'ai vu, {t'a|tu as} vu, on‿a vu.
  • A stem plus an ending. The imparfait and the conditionnel both end in è, or ié with vous. The futur simple takes the conditionnel's stem with endings of its own.
  • A form borrowed from the present. The subjunctive mostly sounds like the ils form.

The infinitive is the form a dictionary lists, and the form that follows another verb: je {peu|peux} partir, tu {veu|veux} manger ? It ends in -er (said é), -ir, -re or -oir. Some verbs need à or de before an infinitive: see the list.

Two forms add an ending to a stem, the imparfait and the conditionnel. Each takes a different stem:

  • Imparfait: take the vous form and change its final é sound to è. vous parlez ("parlé") → je {parlè|parlais} (I was speaking). See 5.5.
  • Conditionnel: take the infinitive and end it in "rè". parler → je {parlerè|parlerais}, said "parl'rè" (I would speak). Verbs with è in the je form keep it: j'achète → j'{achèterè|achèterais}. See 6.3.

So the two differ by one sound, the r: "parlè" / "parl'rè".

With vous, both end in "ié" instead of "è": vous parliez ("parlié"), vous parleriez ("parl'rié").

5.3 Participles

A participle is the form of a verb that describes a finished action or a resulting state, like English "seen", "finished" or "gone". Each verb has one.

Why it matters: learn one participle per verb and you have every compound past. The same participle appears in all of these:

  • j'ai pris (I took): passé composé
  • j'{avè|avais} pris (I had taken): plus-que-parfait (6.2)
  • j'{aurè|aurais} pris (I would have taken): conditionnel passé (6.3)

On its own, a participle works as an adjective, just as in English: la porte {è|est} ouverte (the door is open), c'{è|est} fini (it's finished), {chui|je suis} fatigué (I'm tired).

For -er verbs, which are most verbs, the participle is fully regular. It also sounds exactly like the infinitive and the vous form: parler, vous parlez, j'ai parlé all end in "parlé". Only their written forms differ.

For the rest, the present doesn't predict the participle. This is a real area of irregularity. The verbs in the last two rows are among the most common in the language, so you'll meet them constantly, which makes them easier to learn. There's a fuller list. Verbs built on them follow them: comprendre → compris, apprendre → appris, devenir → devenu, offrir → offert.

5.4 Passé composé: what happened

avoir or être in the present, plus the participle. Only the helper changes, and you know it already:

It covers every English simple past and present perfect: j'ai vu is "I saw", "I've seen" and "I did see".

avoir or être

Most verbs use avoir. Two groups use être, and these you have to learn:

  • A short list of verbs of coming, going and changing state, mostly in pairs: aller / venir, arriver / partir, entrer / sortir, monter / descendre, naître / mourir, plus rester, tomber, passer, retourner, rentrer, devenir, revenir (list with meanings).
  • All reflexive verbs (4.5, list): je me suis levé (I got up), on s'{è|est} {vu|vus} {ièr|hier} (we saw each other yesterday).

You'll also hear j'ai été for "I went": j'ai été à Paris = {chui|je suis} allé à Paris (I went to Paris). In the Paris area it's about as common as the être form. Use {chui|je suis} allé yourself; with y it's almost always j'y suis allé (I went there).

Research: Glikman & Patard 2022.

Pronouns and pas fit around the helper: je l'ai vu (3.5), j'ai pas vu (7.1).

More: être verbs that take avoir

sortir, monter, descendre, passer and rentrer take avoir when they have a direct object, and the meaning shifts:

  • {chui|je suis} sorti (I went out) · j'ai sorti la poubelle (I took the bin out)
  • il {è|est} monté (he went up) · il a monté les {valise|valises} (he carried the cases up)
  • {chui|je suis} passé chez toi (I dropped by your place) · j'ai passé une bonne journée (I had a good day)

When the participle changes

With être, the participle agrees with the subject, like an adjective. Usually you can't hear it: il {è|est} parti, elle {è|est} {parti|partie}, ils sont {parti|partis} all end in "parti". You hear it only on the participles that end in a consonant, which add that consonant for the feminine: il {è|est} mort / elle {è|est} morte.

More: agreement with avoir

With avoir, the participle can also agree with a direct object that comes before the verb: la lettre que j'ai écrite (the letter I wrote), ta veste, je l'ai prise (your jacket, I took it). You only hear it on participles that end in a consonant (prise, mise, faite, écrite, ouverte), and in conversation it's optional: la lettre que j'ai {écrit|écrite} is at least as common, and both sound normal. You hear the agreement more in careful speech, and most when the participle ends the sentence: c'{è|est} une habitude que j'ai prise (it's a habit I've picked up). Written French always makes it (A5).

Research: Gaucher 2013.

5.5 Imparfait: what was going on

Take the present vous form and change its final é sound to è: vous parlez ("parlé") → je {parlè|parlais} ("parlè"). With vous, it ends in "ié": vous parliez ("parlié").

vous (present)je, tu, il, on, ilsvous
vous parlez{parlè|je parlais · tu parlais · il parlait · on parlait · ils parlaient}vous parliez
vous finissez{finissè|je finissais · tu finissais · il finissait · on finissait · ils finissaient}vous finissiez
vous prenez{prenè|je prenais · tu prenais · il prenait · on prenait · ils prenaient}vous preniez
vous‿avez{avè|j'avais · tu avais · il avait · on avait · ils avaient}vous‿aviez
vous‿allez{allè|j'allais · tu allais · il allait · on allait · ils allaient}vous‿alliez

So the imparfait has only two spoken forms. je {parlè|parlais} and ils {parlè|parlaient} are the same.

Three exceptions, because their present vous forms (vous‿êtes, vous faites, vous dites) don't end in é:

Verbje, tu, il, on, ilsvous
être{étè|j'étais · tu étais · il était · on était · ils étaient}vous‿étiez

dire{disè|je disais · tu disais · il disait · on disait · ils disaient}vous disiez

When to use it: for the background, for ongoing states, and for habits. Quand j'{étè|étais} petit, on‿{allè|allait} à la mer (when I was little, we used to go to the seaside). Very frequent forms: c'{étè|était} (it was), {y avè|il y avait} (there was), il fallait (we had to), je {savè|savais} pas (I didn't know).

To soften a request, use the imparfait of vouloir (to want): je {voulè|voulais} vous demander quelque chose (I wanted to ask you something), je {voulè|voulais} savoir si… (I was wondering if…). It sounds less direct than je {veu|veux}. More polite questions are in 8.5.

Passé composé or imparfait?

The passé composé moves the story forward: this happened, then that. The imparfait describes the scene around it.

il pleuvait quand {chui|je suis} sorti

5.6 Futur proche: what's going to happen

aller in the present (4.3) plus an infinitive: je vais manger, tu {va|vas} adorer, on va voir, vous‿allez voir, ils vont venir. This is the everyday way to talk about the future. pas and pronouns go around vais: je vais pas venir, je vais le faire.

You'll also hear a one-word future, the futur simple (6.4), in about four futures out of ten. It's the usual future of a few common verbs, ce sera (it'll be), {y aura|il y aura} (there'll be), and it's common in negatives: ça changera rien (it won't change anything).

dépêche-toi, on va rater le train !

Hurry up, we're going to miss the train! · aller, present, on + infinitive
