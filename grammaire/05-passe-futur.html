Chapter 5 · Verbs IIPast, future and possibility

Spoken French needs about eight verb forms beyond the present. Almost all of them are built from pieces you already have: avoir, être and aller in the present, the infinitive, and the vous and ils forms. Add two endings and one new form, the participle, and you can build the rest. The genuinely irregular parts are few, and this chapter points each one out.

The "built from" column shows three ways of building:

  • A helper verb plus another form. The helper (avoir, être, aller, venir) changes with the person, and you already know it from 4.3. The other part never changes: j'ai vu, {t'as*|tu as} vu, on‿a vu.

  • A form borrowed from the present. The subjunctive mostly sounds like the ils form.

5.3 Participles

A participle is the form of a verb that describes a finished action or a resulting state, like English "seen", "finished" or "gone". Each verb has one.

Why it matters: learn one participle per verb and you have every compound past. The same participle appears in all of these:

  • j'ai pris (I took): passé composé
  • j'avais pris (I had taken): plus-que-parfait
  • j'aurais pris (I would have taken): conditionnel passé

On its own, a participle works as an adjective, just as in English: la porte est‿ouverte (the door is open), c'est fini (it's finished), {chui*|je suis} fatigué (I'm tired).

How to form it

For -er verbs, which are most verbs, the participle is fully regular. It also sounds exactly like the infinitive and the vous form: parler, vous parlez, j'ai parlé all end in "parlé". Only their written forms differ.

For the rest, the present doesn't predict the participle. This is a real area of irregularity. The verbs in the last two rows are among the most common in the language, so you'll meet them constantly, which makes them easier to learn. Verbs built on them follow them: comprendre → compris, apprendre → appris, devenir → devenu, offrir → offert.

5.4 Passé composé: what happened

avoir or être in the present, plus the participle. Only the helper changes, and you know it already:

Whowith avoirwith être
jej'ai mangé{chui*|je suis} parti
tu{t'as*|tu as} mangé{t'es*|tu es} parti
il, elle, onil a mangé · on‿a mangéil est parti · on‿est partis
vousvous‿avez mangévous‿êtes partis
ilsils‿ont mangéils sont partis

It covers every English simple past and present perfect: j'ai vu is "I saw", "I've seen" and "I did see".

avoir or être

Most verbs use avoir. Two groups use être, and these you have to learn:

  • A short list of verbs of coming, going and changing state, mostly in pairs: aller / venir, arriver / partir, entrer / sortir, monter / descendre, naître / mourir, plus rester, tomber, passer, retourner, rentrer, devenir, revenir.
  • All reflexive verbs (4.5): je me suis levé (I got up), on s'est vus {ièr*|hier} (we saw each other yesterday).

Pronouns and pas fit around the helper: je l'ai vu (3.5), j'ai pas vu (6.1).

More: être verbs that take avoir

sortir, monter, descendre, passer and rentrer take avoir when they have a direct object, and the meaning shifts:

  • {chui*|je suis} sorti (I went out) · j'ai sorti la poubelle (I took the bin out)
  • il est monté (he went up) · il a monté les valises (he carried the cases up)
  • {chui*|je suis} passé chez toi (I dropped by your place) · j'ai passé une bonne journée (I had a good day)

When the participle changes

With être, the participle agrees with the subject, like an adjective. Usually you can't hear it: il est parti, elle est partie, ils sont partis all end in "parti". You hear it only on the participles that end in a consonant, which add that consonant for the feminine: il est mort / elle est morte.

More: agreement with avoir

With avoir, the participle agrees with a direct object that comes before the verb: la lettre que j'ai écrite, ta veste, je l'ai prise (your jacket, I took it). This is only audible on consonant-ending participles (prise, mise, faite, écrite, ouverte), and many speakers leave it out in casual speech: la lettre que j'ai écrit. Written French always makes it (A5).

on‿est rentrés tard

We got home late. · rentrer, passé composé with être, on

5.5 Imparfait: what was going on

Take the present vous form, drop its final é, and add è. With vous, add ié instead:

vous (present)je, tu, il, on, ilsvous
vous parlezje parlais · ils {parlè*|parlaient}vous parliez
vous finissezje finissaisvous finissiez
vous prenezje prenaisvous preniez
vous‿avezj'avaisvous‿aviez
vous‿allezj'allaisvous‿alliez

So the imparfait has only two spoken forms. je parlais and ils {parlè*|parlaient} sound the same.

Three exceptions, because their vous forms don't end in é:

  • être: j'étais, c'était, on‿était
  • faire: je {fesais*|faisais}
  • dire: je disais

When to use it: for the background, for ongoing states, and for habits. Quand j'étais petit, on‿allait à la mer (when I was little, we used to go to the seaside). Very frequent forms: c'était (it was), {y avait*|il y avait} (there was), il fallait (we had to), je savais pas (I didn't know).

Passé composé or imparfait?

The passé composé moves the story forward: this happened, then that. The imparfait describes the scene around it.

il pleuvait quand {chui*|je suis} sorti

Il pleuvait quand je suis sorti.

It was raining when I went out. · pleuvoir, imparfait (scene) · sortir, passé composé (event)

  2. vous‿habitez → on ___ on‿habitait
  3. vous voulez → je ___ je voulais
  4. vous‿êtes → c'___ c'était (être is an exception)
  5. vous faites → je ___ je {fesais*|faisais} (exception)
  6. vous venez → vous ___ vous veniez

5.6 Plus-que-parfait: what had already happened

The passé composé with its helper moved into the imparfait. You already have both pieces:

  • j'ai oublié (I forgot) → j'avais oublié (I had forgotten)
  • il est parti (he left) → il était déjà parti (he had already left)
  • on s'est pas compris → on s'était pas compris (we hadn't understood each other)

The choice of avoir or être is the same as in the passé composé.

5.7 Futur proche: what's going to happen

aller in the present plus an infinitive: je vais manger, on va voir, tu vas adorer, ils vont venir. This is the everyday way to talk about the future. pas and pronouns go around vais: je vais pas venir, je vais le faire.

Two more combinations work the same way: venir de + infinitive for what just happened (il vient de partir, he's just left), and être en train de + infinitive for what's happening right now ({chui*|je suis} en train de manger, I'm eating right now).

More: the simple future

French also has a one-word future. It uses the same stem as the conditionnel (5.8), with different endings: je parlerai, tu parleras, on parlera, vous parlerez, ils parleront. In conversation it's mostly found in a few fixed phrases. Learn these as vocabulary:

on verra (we'll see) · ça ira (it'll be OK) · ce sera (it will be) · tu verras (you'll see) · on‿en reparlera (we'll talk about it later)

5.8 Conditionnel: what would happen

Take the infinitive (without the final e of -re verbs) and add the imparfait endings, è and ié:

Infinitiveje, tu, il, on, ilsvous
parlerje parlerais ("parl'rè") · ils {parlerè*|parleraient}vous parleriez
finirje finiraisvous finiriez
prendreje prendraisvous prendriez

So the conditionnel always has an r before the ending. That r is what tells je parlerais (I would speak) from je parlais (I was speaking).

5.9 Subjunctive

When: after a short list of phrases. The main ones are il faut que (it's necessary that), je veux que (I want…to), j'aimerais que (I'd like…to), pour que (so that), avant que (before), bien que (although) and ça m'étonnerait que (I'd be surprised if). The subjunctive rarely adds meaning of its own. It's required by the phrase, so learn the phrases.

How it sounds: for almost every verb, the subjunctive with je, tu, il, on or ils sounds like the present-tense ils form:

ils (present)Subjunctive
ils {parte*|partent}il faut qu'on parte
ils {finisse*|finissent}il faut que je finisse
ils {prenne*|prennent}il faut que tu prennes
ils {vienne*|viennent}j'aimerais qu'il vienne
ils {parle*|parlent}il faut qu'on parle

For one-form verbs (4.1), which includes every -er verb, the ils form sounds like the je form. So il faut que je parle sounds just like the present je parle, and you'll often use the subjunctive without noticing.

With vous, use the imparfait vous form (5.5): il faut que vous partiez.

Six common verbs have their own subjunctive, and these you have to learn. Learn them with on:
