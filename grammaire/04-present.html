Chapter 4Verbs: the present

In the present, a verb has at most three spoken forms, and most verbs have two. Each is written one way, so what you see is what you say: je parle, tu {parle|parles}, ils {parle|parlent}. "We" is on, which uses the same form as il and elle.

The pronouns themselves, and when to say tu or vous, are in chapter 3. Orange words are spelled differently in standard French: tap one to see how (1.1).

4.1 One-form verbs

Most verbs, including every verb whose infinitive (dictionary form) ends in -er, have one form for everyone except vous. The vous form adds é.

WhoSay
je, tu, il, elle, on, ils, elles{parle|je parle · tu parles · il parle · elle parle · on parle · ils parlent · elles parlent}
vousvous parlez

All the forms except vous are the same word, parle. The same goes for on mange, je travaille, tu {regarde|regardes}, ils‿{aime|aiment}.

on regarde un film ?

Shall we watch a film? · regarder, present, on

One or many?

With one-form verbs, il parle and ils {parle|parlent} are the same. Listeners tell them apart from the article on the subject or from context: le voisin parle / les {voisin|voisins} {parle|parlent}. Before a vowel, ils links: il aime / ils‿{aime|aiment}.

Vowel shift in the vous form

A few verbs have a clear è in the main form that becomes a light e or é in the vous form: j'achète / vous achetez, j'appelle / vous appelez, je préfère / vous préférez.

Other one-form verbs

A handful of verbs from other groups work the same way:

4.2 Two-stem verbs

The other verbs have two stems:

  • Long stem, ending in a consonant you hear: ils and elles.
  • Short stem, the long stem without that consonant: je, tu, il, elle, on.
  • vous: the long stem plus é.

So you learn one form per verb, the ils form, and work out the rest. The same pattern links feminine and masculine adjectives (petite → petit); see 2.6.

4.3 The six core verbs

These six come up constantly. Learn them as whole sets.

Verbjetuil, elle, onvousils
être{chui|je suis}{t'è|tu es}{è|il est · elle est · on est}vous‿êtesils sont
avoirj'ai{t'a|tu as}avous‿avezils‿ont
allerje vaistu {va|vas}vavous‿allezils vont
faireje {fè|fais}tu {fè|fais}{fè|il fait · elle fait · on fait}vous faitesils font

{chui|je suis} and {chais|je sais} are je run together with the verb: the j and the s merge into "ch" (1.7).

Three contrasts to train your ear on:

  • ils sont (they are) versus ils‿ont (they have): "il son" versus "il zon". Only s versus z.
  • {t'a|tu as}, il a, on‿a: the same a, written the same; only the pronoun tells them apart.
  • on‿{è|est}, on‿a, on va, on {fè|fait}: "we are, we have, we're going, we're doing". Make these automatic first.

Some verbs only appear with il or ça: il faut, il pleut, ça va, ça {dépen|dépend}. In speech, il faut usually drops its il: {faut|il faut} partir (1.7).

Many everyday expressions use avoir or faire where English uses "be" or "do": j'ai faim (I'm hungry), il {fè|fait} beau (the weather's nice). See the list.

{chais|je sais} pas, on verra

Je sais pas, on verra.

I don't know, we'll see. · savoir, present, je · voir, future, on

savoir or connaître?

Both mean "know". connaître (ils {connaisse|connaissent}, 4.2) is for people, places and things you're familiar with. savoir is for facts and know-how:

  • connaître + a person, a place or a thing: tu {connè|connais} Paul ? (do you know Paul?), je {connè|connais} bien Lyon, tu {connè|connais} ce resto ? (do you know this restaurant?)
  • savoir + que, si or a question word: je {sè|sais} qu'il {è|est} là (I know he's here), {chais|je sais} pas où il habite (8.4).
  • savoir + an infinitive means "know how to": tu {sè|sais} nager ? (can you swim?)
  • savoir on its own: je {sè|sais}, {chais|je sais} pas, on {sè|sait} jamais (you never know).

A quick test: if a noun follows, it's usually connaître.

4.4 Commands

A command is the tu or vous form on its own: {Vien|Viens} ! Venez ! {Atten|Attends} ! Regarde ! For "let's", use on: On‿y va ! (You'll also hear allons‿y.)

être has its own command forms: {Soi|Sois} sage ! Soyez {sage|sages} ! A pronoun goes after the verb: Donne-moi ça. {Vas‿y|Vas-y}. In a negative command, it goes before: T'inquiète pas. T'en {fè|fais} pas.

4.5 Reflexive verbs

Reflexive verbs have an extra pronoun before the verb, matching the subject:

WhoPronounExample
jeme (m')je me lève · je m'appelle Paul
tute (t')tu te {lève|lèves} · tu t'{appelle|appelles} comment ?
il, elle, onse (s')il se lève · on se {voi|voit} demain
vousvousvous vous levez · vous vous connaissez ?
ils, ellesse (s')ils se {lève|lèvent} · ils s'{appelle|appellent} comment ?

With on, the pronoun is se, and it often means "each other": on se {voi|voit} demain (we'll see each other tomorrow). So does vous: vous vous connaissez ? (do you know each other?) In commands, the pronoun goes after the verb, and te becomes toi: lève-toi ! (get up!), dépêchez-vous ! (hurry up!). Common reflexive verbs are in the list. With parts of the body, the reflexive pronoun shows whose: je me lave les {main|mains} (I'm washing my hands; 2.7).

4.6 devoir: have to, must

devoir (je {doi|dois}, tu {doi|dois}, on {doi|doit}, vous devez, ils {doive|doivent}: 4.2) has two meanings, like English "must":

  • Have to: je {doi|dois} partir (I have to go), on {doi|doit} payer maintenant ? (do we have to pay now?)
  • Must be, probably: a guess about what's likely: il {doi|doit} être malade (he must be ill), ça {doi|doit} être cher (that must be expensive), elle {doi|doit} être chez‿elle (she must be at home).

For a general need, speech often uses {faut|il faut} + infinitive instead: {faut|il faut} partir (we have to go). To say who, use {faut|il faut} que (6.5).

In the passé composé (5.4), j'ai dû keeps both meanings: j'ai dû partir tôt (I had to leave early), j'ai dû oublier (I must have forgotten). Context tells you which. The conditionnel gives "should": tu {devrè|devrais} (you should), j'{aurè|aurais} dû (I should have) (6.3).
