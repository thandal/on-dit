AppendixComing from written French

A0. General

This appendix is for readers who learned written French first. Each section maps one chapter onto the written forms you already know.

Spoken French has far fewer distinct forms than written French, mainly for five reasons:

  1. Silent endings. Final -e, -es, -ent, -s, -t, -x and -d are mostly silent, so forms spelled differently sound alike.
  2. on replaces nous as the subject in conversation. nous survives as an emphatic pronoun (Nous, on part demain) and in formal speech.
  3. Some tenses are written-only: the passé simple, the passé antérieur, and the imperfect and pluperfect subjunctive.
  4. ne is normally dropped in neutral informal speech: je sais pas, c'est pas grave. Keeping it sounds formal or emphatic.
  5. Inverted questions are rare in conversation (Où vas-tu ?). Intonation and question words in place are the default: tu vas où ?

A1. The spoken spelling

Standard spelling often writes one sound several ways, depending on grammar: parle, parles, parlent; peux, peut; livre, livres. This grammar writes each spoken form one way, and shows every word written differently from standard spelling in orange:

  • Verb forms that sound the same are written as they sound, with no silent letters: je peux, il peut → je {peu|peux}, il {peu|peut}; je fais, il fait → je {fè|fais}, il {fè|fait}; tu es, il est → {t'è|tu es}, il {è|est}. Where one standard spelling already reads right, it's used for all: tu parles, ils parlent → tu {parle|parles}, ils {parle|parlent}.
  • Silent plural and feminine endings are left off: les livres → les {livre|livres}, elle est fatiguée → elle {è|est} {fatigué|fatiguée}. Where linking makes the ending heard, it stays: les petits‿{enfant|enfants}.
  • Forms the rules can't predict are respelled: ils partent → ils {parte|partent} (read by the rules, -ent would be "an").
  • Relaxed forms: je suis → {chui|je suis}.
  • Irregular words: femme → {fam|femme}.

Tap any orange word, or turn on Written forms, to see its standard spelling. In a table that covers several persons, the tap shows each person's spelling.

Linking (liaison) is written with a tie instead of a respelling, because the word itself doesn't change. The grammar only shows obligatory linking, plus a few very common optional ones.

Respellings use ordinary French letters. To write standard French, you learn the spellings behind the orange words: mostly the silent endings that mark person, number and gender.

A2. Nouns, articles, gender and number

Silent plural endings. Written nouns and adjectives add -s or -x in the plural, but these are silent except in linking: les livres, des voitures rouges.

Silent agreement. Written adjectives add -e for the feminine and -s for the plural. For adjectives like joli, vrai, noir, all four written forms sound the same: joli, jolie, jolis, jolies. The feminine is only audible when the -e follows a consonant that is otherwise silent: petit / petite.

de before a plural adjective. Written French uses de instead of des before an adjective: de bons amis, de grandes maisons. In conversation you'll often hear des.

Comparisons (2.9). Spelling doesn't show the two sounds of plus: plus grand ("plu") and il travaille plus ("pluss") are written alike. Careful writing adds ne after a comparison with a clause: c'est plus cher que je ne pensais. moindre (le moindre problème, the slightest problem) and plus mauvais for pire are more common in writing.

A3. Pronouns

nous as a subject. Written French uses nous with the -ons verb form: nous partons. In conversation, on replaces it: on {par|part}.

tu doesn't shorten in writing. tu es, tu as are written in full; {t'è|tu es}, {t'a|tu as} are spoken forms.

cela and ceci are the written forms of ça. In writing, cela is used where speech has ça: Cela dépend.

Two object pronouns (3.5). Written French keeps both, in a fixed order: Je le lui ai donné. In speech, le, la and les usually drop before lui and leur.

ne with pronouns. Written negatives put ne before the object pronouns: Je ne le connais pas, Il n'y en a plus. Speech drops ne: je le {connè|connais} pas.

ce sont. Before a plural, careful writing uses ce sont: Ce sont mes amis. Speech mostly keeps c'est: c'{è|est} mes‿{ami|amis}.

celui-ci, celle-ci ("this one") contrast with celui-là ("that one") in writing. Speech uses celui-là for both (3.8).

le nôtre, le vôtre (3.9) take a circumflex; notre, votre (2.7) don't.

tous is one spelling for two sounds (3.10): "tou" before a noun (tous les jours), "touss" on its own (ils sont tous là).

A4. Verbs: the present

Six written forms, two spoken ones. For -er verbs, the written endings -e, -es, -e and -ent are all silent, and on replaces nous:

WrittenSpoken
je parle, tu parles, il parle, ils parlent"parl"
nous parlonsreplaced by on parle
vous parlez"parlé"

nous forms (parlons, finissons, sommes): recognize them in formal speech, the news and writing.

The two-stem rule in written terms. The written singular endings (-s, -s, -t) are silent. In the ils form, -ent is silent but the consonant before it is said. That consonant is the whole spoken difference: il part / ils partent, il finit / ils finissent.

Spelling-change -er verbs (acheter, appeler, jeter, préférer): the accent or double consonant records the vowel shift described in 4.1.

Commands. The written tu command of -er verbs drops its s (Parle !), but restores it before y and en (Vas-y, Parles-en), where it's heard as z. Written negative commands keep ne: Ne t'inquiète pas.

When to learn the spelling distinctions. parle / parles / parlent sound the same. Learn which to write when you start writing.

A5. Verbs: the everyday past and future

Written-only past tenses. Recognize these when reading; don't use them in conversation.

FormExampleWhere you'll meet it
passé simpleil fut, ils allèrentliterature, history, journalism
passé antérieurquand il eut finiliterature

In writing, the passé simple tells a story where speech uses the passé composé.

Participle agreement (5.4). Written French makes participles agree with the subject after être, and with a preceding direct object after avoir. Agreement is only audible on participles that end in a consonant in the feminine (mise, prise, faite, écrite, ouverte). After avoir, speech makes it only some of the time, even there: la lettre que j'ai {écrit|écrite} is as common as la lettre que j'ai écrite, but writing always has écrite.

A6. Verbs: other common forms

Written-only or rare in speech. Recognize these when reading.

FormExampleWhere you'll meet it
imperfect subjunctivequ'il fûtliterary and older texts
pluperfect subjunctivequ'il eût faitliterary and older texts
futur antérieurj'aurai finiwriting; occasional in speech

Futur simple. More frequent in writing and formal speech. In conversation, the futur proche is the default, but the futur simple is still about four futures in ten: it's the usual future of être, avoir, pouvoir, savoir and falloir, and common in negatives (6.4).

Conditionnel and futur. je voudrais and je voudrai are distinct in writing but sound the same for many speakers.

Subjunctive endings. The written rule (ils stem + -e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent) gives the same spoken result as 6.5. The nous form (-ions) is replaced by on. A few rarer verbs also have their own subjunctive, such as vouloir (qu'il veuille) and falloir (qu'il faille).

A7. Negation

ne is always written. Written negation has two parts: je ne sais pas, il n'y a plus de pain, je n'ai rien vu. Speech keeps only the second part.

ne… que is the written form of "only": je n'ai que dix euros.

Written-only negatives. ne… guère (hardly), ne… point (not at all) and a lone ne without pas (je ne sais, avant qu'il ne parte) belong to literary or very formal style.

ne pas before an infinitive. Both words go before it in writing: il m'a dit de ne pas venir.

A8. Questions

Inversion is the standard written question: Où vas-tu ? Quand part-il ? Vient-elle ? With a noun subject, a pronoun is added: Paul vient-il ? A t is inserted between two vowels: A-t-il fini ?

est-ce que is acceptable in writing and is the neutral written alternative to inversion.

Question words at the end (tu vas où ?) and quoi as a direct object are informal and appear in writing only in dialogue or messages.

Que is the written "what" at the start of an inverted question: Que fais-tu ?

A9. Sentence shape

Topic-first sentences (Mon frère, il habite à Lyon) are rare in formal writing, which uses the plain order: Mon frère habite à Lyon.

il y a is written in full: il y a, il n'y a pas.

voici (here is) is used in writing alongside voilà; in speech, voilà covers both.

A10. Joining ideas

More joining words in writing: lorsque (when), puisque (since), tandis que (while, whereas), afin que (so that), cependant (however), toutefois (however), ainsi (thus).

dont meaning "whose" is mainly written: un auteur dont le livre… (an author whose book…). In speech, dont goes mostly with verbs that take de (10.4).

lequel, laquelle, lesquels, lesquelles follow a preposition when the noun is a thing: la raison pour laquelle… (the reason why…). Speech tends to rephrase.

après + past infinitive: après avoir mangé (after eating), après être parti.

Reported speech follows the tense shift strictly in writing: il a dit qu'il viendrait (future → conditionnel).

A11. Discourse words

Most of chapter 11 doesn't appear in writing outside dialogue and messages. Written French has its own linking words instead: en effet (indeed), cependant (however), par ailleurs (moreover), en outre (furthermore), néanmoins (nevertheless), ainsi (thus), donc (therefore).

Spellings you'll see in messages: ben, bah, ouais, du coup, genre, and mdr, short for mort de rire (dying of laughter, the French "lol").

A12. Everyday frames

Numbers are usually written as digits. Written number words join with hyphens: vingt-deux, quatre-vingt-dix. quatre-vingts and deux cents take s only when nothing follows: quatre-vingt-trois, deux cent dix.

Decimals and prices use a comma: 7,50 €. Large numbers use a space: 1 000 000.

Times are written with h and the 24-hour clock: 17 h 30, 9 h.

Dates are written le 1er mai, le 14 juillet, with no capitals on days or months.

Ordinals are written with a small e: 2e, 3e, 21e (also 2ème). "First" is 1er, or 1re for the feminine. Centuries often use Roman numerals: le XXIe siècle.

A13. Place and time

ça fait… que (13.5) is written cela fait… que, or voilà… que and il y a… que: Voilà deux ans que j'habite ici.

More time words in writing: durant (during, for: the written pendant), lors de (at the time of), dès (from, as early as: dès lundi).

More place words in writing: auprès de (with, next to) and au sein de (within).

Spelling. pied (13.4) ends in a silent d, and its -ied is said "ié". longtemps has a silent g.
