How this grammar works

This grammar teaches French as people in France speak it day to day, with friends, family and colleagues.

Scope

  • Variety: contemporary France French.
  • Register: neutral informal, the everyday speech of educated adults: {chais*|je sais} pas, on‿y va, {t'es*|tu es} où ? Slang appears only in occasional "you'll hear" notes.
  • Each chapter gives the forms you hear and say, examples, and exercises.
  • Already know written French? The appendix compares the two, one section per chapter.

Reading the examples

Every French example is written so you can read it aloud correctly using the rules in chapter 1. Most words keep their normal spelling. A word whose sound you couldn't work out from the rules is respelled and gets a star: ils {parte*|partent}.

To see the standard spelling of starred words, turn on Written forms at the top of the page, or tap any starred word.

ils {parte*|partent} demain

Ils partent demain.

They're leaving tomorrow. · partir, present, ils

Key examples look like this: what you say, what's written (shown with Written forms on), and what it means and which form it is.

Principles

  1. Sound comes first. Rules describe what you hear and say.
  2. One default per job. Each chapter teaches the most common way to say something. Other forms you may hear are listed briefly, for recognition.
  3. Speech has its own structures, and they are core grammar here: relaxed forms, dislocation (moi, je…), clefts (c'est lui qui…) and discourse words.
  4. Recognize more than you produce. You'll hear more forms than you need to use.

Chapter 1Sounds and spelling

Learn these rules once and you can read every example in this grammar aloud. Unstarred words are also spelled the standard way, so you learn to say and write them at the same time.

1.1 Three marks

*

Respelled. The word is spelled the way it sounds. Tap it to see the standard spelling: {chui*|je suis}.

‿

Linked. The first word's last consonant is said at the start of the next: les‿amis sounds like "lé-zami".

abc

Unmarked. Read it with the rules below. That's also how it's written.

1.3 Nasal vowels

A vowel followed by n or m in the same syllable is nasal: air goes through the nose, and the n or m itself isn't said.

LettersSoundExamples
an, am, en, emansans, temps, enfant
in, im, ain, ein, uninvin, pain, plein, un
on, omonbon, nom
ieni + inbien, viens

Before a vowel, or when the n or m is doubled, the vowel is not nasal and the n or m is said: bonne, ami, année, vienne.

1.4 Consonants

  • c is k before a, o, u or a consonant, and s before e, i, y: café, ici. ç is always s: ça.
  • g is g before a, o, u, and j before e, i: gare, rouge. gu before e or i is g: guide.
  • ch is sh; gn is ny; qu is k; ph is f; th is t: chat, montagne, qui, photo, thé.
  • h is never said: hôtel.
  • s between two vowels is z; ss is s: maison, poisson.
  • ill after a vowel, and a final il after a vowel, is y: travail, travailler, aille.
  • y between two vowels is like i + y: vous voyez sounds like "vwa-yé".

1.5 Ends of words

  • Final s, t, d, x, z, p are silent: pas, part, grand, deux, nez, trop.
  • Final c, r, f, l are said: avec, pour, neuf, il. The exception is final -er in a word of two or more syllables, which is é: parler, premier.
  • A consonant before a final e is said: il part ends in r, but qu'on parte ends in t.
  • Final -es is silent, the same as final -e: tu parles sounds like il parle.
  • Final -ent is an: moment, souvent.

1.6 Linking

The tie ‿ links two words. The consonant at the end of the first word is said at the start of the second: s, x and z are said as z, d as t, and n as n.

ExampleSounds like
les‿amislé-zami
vous‿avezvou-zavé
ils‿ontil-zon
on‿y vaon-ni va
quand‿ilkan-til

Linking happens most after the little words that come before a noun or verb: les, des, ces, mes, un, deux, trois, on, nous, vous, ils, elles, en, très. The tie is always shown, so you never have to guess.

Chapter 4 · Verbs IThe present

In the present, a verb has at most three spoken forms, and most verbs have two. "We" is on, which uses the same form as il and elle.

4.1 One-form verbs

Most verbs, including every verb whose infinitive ends in -er, have one form for everyone except vous. The vous form adds é.

WhoSayWritten
je, tu, il, elle, onje parle · tu parles · il parle · on parleje parle, tu parles, il parle, on parle
ils, ellesils {parle*|parlent}ils parlent
vousvous parlezvous parlez

All the forms except vous sound like "parl". The same goes for on mange, je travaille, tu regardes, ils {aime*|aiment}.

on regarde un film ?

Shall we watch a film? · regarder, present, on

One or many?

With one-form verbs, il parle and ils {parle*|parlent} sound the same. Listeners tell them apart from the article on the subject or from context: le voisin parle / les voisins {parle*|parlent}. Before a vowel, ils links: il aime / ils‿{aime*|aiment}.

Vowel shift in the vous form

A few verbs have a clear è in the main form that becomes a light e or é in the vous form: j'achète / vous achetez, j'appelle / vous appelez, je préfère / vous préférez.

4.3 The six core verbs

These six come up constantly. Learn them as whole sets.

Three contrasts to train your ear on:

  • ils sont (they are) versus ils‿ont (they have): "il son" versus "il zon". Only s versus z.
  • {t'as*|tu as}, il a, on‿a: the same a; only the pronoun tells them apart.
  • on‿est, on‿a, on va, on fait: "we are, we have, we're going, we're doing". Make these automatic first.

4.4 Commands

A command is the tu or vous form on its own: Viens ! Venez ! Attends ! Regarde ! For "let's", use on: On‿y va ! (You'll also hear allons‿y.)

être has its own command forms: Sois sage ! Soyez sages ! A pronoun goes after the verb: Donne-moi ça. {Vas‿y*|Vas-y}. In a negative command, it goes before: T'inquiète pas. T'en fais pas.

4.5 Reflexive verbs

Reflexive verbs add me, te or se before the verb: je me lève, tu t'appelles comment ?, on se voit demain. With on, the pronoun is se; with vous, it's vous: vous vous connaissez ?

5.2 Passé composé: avoir or être + past participle

Participles by sound

  • -er verbs end in é: parlé, mangé, allé. Same sound as parler and vous parlez.
  • Most -ir verbs end in i: fini, parti, dormi, sorti.
  • Many others end in u: vu, lu, bu, {u*|eu}, su, pu, voulu, venu, connu, attendu, répondu.
  • Learn these as words: été (être), fait, dit, pris, mis, écrit, ouvert, mort, né.

avoir or être

Most verbs use avoir. être goes with all reflexive verbs and with these: aller, venir, partir, arriver, sortir, entrer, rentrer, rester, tomber, monter, descendre, passer, retourner, devenir, naître, mourir.

One form for everyone

A participle sounds the same whoever the subject is: il est parti, elle est partie, ils sont partis all end in "parti". A few participles that end in a consonant add it for a woman or a feminine noun: elle est morte, la porte est ouverte.

on‿est rentrés tard

We got home late. · rentrer, passé composé with être, on

5.3 Imparfait: two forms

Take the vous form of the present, drop the é, and add è. vous adds ié instead.

WhoSayWritten
je, tu, il, onje parlais · tu parlais · il parlait · on parlaitje parlais, tu parlais, il parlait, on parlait
ilsils {parlè*|parlaient}ils parlaient
vousvous parliezvous parliez

The same rule works for every verb: vous finissez → je finissais, vous prenez → je prenais, vous faites → je {fesais*|faisais}. The one exception is être: c'était, j'étais, on‿était.

Use it for background, ongoing states and habits: Quand j'étais petit, on‿allait à la mer. Frequent forms: c'était, {y avait*|il y avait}, il fallait, je savais pas.

Passé composé or imparfait?

The passé composé moves the story forward; the imparfait describes the scene around it.

il pleuvait quand {chui*|je suis} sorti

Il pleuvait quand je suis sorti.

It was raining when I went out. · pleuvoir, imparfait (scene) · sortir, passé composé (event)

5.4 Plus-que-parfait

Imparfait of avoir or être plus a participle: j'avais oublié, il était déjà parti, on s'était pas compris.

5.5 Futur proche

aller in the present plus an infinitive: je vais manger, on va voir, tu vas adorer, ils vont venir. This is the everyday way to talk about the future.

A few fixed phrases use a separate future form. Learn them as vocabulary: on verra, ça ira, ce sera, tu verras, on‿en reparlera.

5.6 Conditionnel

The conditionnel ends in r + è:

  • Regular verbs: the infinitive (without a final e) plus è: parler → je parlerais ("parl'rè"), finir → je finirais, prendre → je prendrais. With ils: ils {parlerè*|parleraient}.
  • vous: ié instead of è: vous pourriez, vous voudriez.

These forms do most of the work. Learn them as words:

SayUse
je voudraispolite request: I'd like
j'aimeraiswish: I'd like to
tu pourrais · vous pourriezpolite request: could you
on devrait · tu devraisadvice: should
il faudraitadvice: we'd need to
ce seraitevaluation: it would be
ça iraitthat would work
j'irais · je ferais · j'aurais · je seraishypothesis: I'd go, do, have, be

For hypotheses, use si + imparfait, then the conditionnel:

si j'avais le temps, j'irais

If I had time, I'd go. · si + avoir, imparfait · aller, conditionnel

About the past: si j'avais su, je serais venu.

5.7 Subjunctive

When: after a short list of phrases. The main ones are il faut que, je veux que, j'aimerais que, pour que, avant que, bien que and ça m'étonnerait que.

How it sounds: for almost every verb, the subjunctive with je, tu, il, on or ils sounds like the present-tense ils form.

ils (present)Subjunctive

The subjunctive forms need no stars: they're spelled the way they sound.

With vous, use the imparfait vous form: il faut que vous partiez.

Six common verbs have their own subjunctive. Learn them with on: qu'on soit (être), qu'on‿ait (avoir), qu'on‿aille (aller), qu'on fasse (faire), qu'on puisse (pouvoir), qu'on sache (savoir).

Appendix · Coming from written FrenchA0. General

This appendix is for readers who learned written French first. Each section maps one chapter onto the written forms you already know.

Spoken French has far fewer distinct forms than written French, mainly for five reasons:

  1. Silent endings. Final -e, -es, -ent, -s, -t, -x and -d are mostly silent, so forms spelled differently sound alike.
  2. on replaces nous as the subject in conversation. nous survives as an emphatic pronoun (Nous, on part demain) and in formal speech.
  3. Some tenses are written-only: the passé simple, the passé antérieur, and the imperfect and pluperfect subjunctive.
  4. ne is normally dropped in neutral informal speech: je sais pas, c'est pas grave. Keeping it sounds formal or emphatic.
  5. Inverted questions are rare in conversation (Où vas-tu ?). Intonation and question words in place are the default: tu vas où ?

A1. The spoken spelling

The examples use standard spelling wherever French spelling-to-sound rules give the right pronunciation, and respell only where they don't. In practice, the starred words are the places where standard spelling depends on grammar rather than sound:

  • Silent verb endings: ils partent → ils {parte*|partent}. Read by the rules, -ent would be "an".
  • Relaxed forms: je suis → {chui*|je suis}.
  • Irregular words: femme → {fam*|femme}.

Linking (liaison) is written with a tie instead of a respelling, because the word itself doesn't change. The grammar only shows obligatory linking, plus a few very common optional ones.

Respellings use ordinary French letters, so a reader who later learns standard spelling only has to learn the starred words.

A4. The present

Six written forms, two spoken ones. For -er verbs, the written endings -e, -es, -e and -ent are all silent, and on replaces nous:

WrittenSpoken
je parle, tu parles, il parle, ils parlent"parl"
nous parlonsreplaced by on parle
vous parlez"parlé"

nous forms (parlons, finissons, sommes): recognize them in formal speech, the news and writing.

The two-stem rule in written terms. The written singular endings (-s, -s, -t) are silent. In the ils form, -ent is silent but the consonant before it is said. That consonant is the whole spoken difference: il part / ils partent, il finit / ils finissent.

Spelling-change -er verbs (acheter, appeler, jeter, préférer): the accent or double consonant records the vowel shift described in 4.1.

Commands. The written tu command of -er verbs drops its s (Parle !), but restores it before y and en (Vas-y, Parles-en), where it's heard as z. Written negative commands keep ne: Ne t'inquiète pas.

When to learn the spelling distinctions. parle / parles / parlent sound the same. Learn which to write when you start writing.

Futur simple. Common in writing and formal speech. In conversation, the futur proche is the default, and the futur simple appears mostly in fixed phrases and promises.

Participle agreement. Written French makes participles agree with the subject after être, and with a preceding direct object after avoir. Agreement is only audible on participles that end in a consonant in the feminine (mise, prise, faite, écrite, ouverte). After avoir, many speakers leave it out even there: la lettre que j'ai écrit.

Conditionnel and futur. je voudrais and je voudrai are distinct in writing but sound the same for many speakers.

Subjunctive endings. The written rule (ils stem + -e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent) gives the same spoken result as chapter 5. The nous form (-ions) is replaced by on. A few rarer verbs also have their own subjunctive, such as vouloir (qu'il veuille) and falloir (qu'il faille).
